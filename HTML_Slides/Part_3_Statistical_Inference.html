Random variables, expected value and variance

Random variable

A random variable $X$ assigns a number to every outcome of a random experiment (e.g. the fill volume of the next bottle). Its distribution says which values occur with which probability.

Expected value – probability-weighted mean:

\[ \mu=E(X)=\sum_i x_i\,P(X=x_i) \]

Variance – mean squared deviation from $\mu$:

\[ \sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(X)=E\bigl[(X-\mu)^2\bigr]=E(X^2)-\mu^2 \]

Continuous $X$ with density $f$: sums become integrals, e.g. $E(X)=\int x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

Complaints per day in an online shop

$x$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$P(X=x)$$0.1$$0.3$$0.4$$0.2$

$E(X)=0\cdot0.1+1\cdot0.3+2\cdot0.4+3\cdot0.2=\mathbf{1.7}$

$E(X^2)=0+0.3+1.6+1.8=3.7$

$\mathrm{Var}(X)=3.7-1.7^2=\mathbf{0.81}$,  $\sigma=\mathbf{0.9}$

In the long run: $1.7$ complaints per day on average – a value that no single day can have.

Two rules that explain why averaging helps

Linear transformation

\begin{align*} E(a+bX)&=a+b\,E(X)\\ \mathrm{Var}(a+bX)&=b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X) \end{align*}

Sums

\begin{align*} E(X+Y)&=E(X)+E(Y) && \text{\scriptsize always}\\ \mathrm{Var}(X+Y)&=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y) && \text{\scriptsize if independent} \end{align*}

Applied to the sample mean of i.i.d. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ with $E(X_i)=\mu$, $\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2$:

\[ E(\bar X)=\frac1n\cdot n\mu=\mu,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

$\bar X$ is right on average, and its variance shrinks like $1/n$: the variances of the $n$ values add up to $n\sigma^2$, but averaging divides by $n^2$. (Variances add – standard deviations do not.)

Talk track: two lines of algebra carry the whole part. Write $\bar X=\tfrac1n(X_1+\dots+X_n)$ on the board, apply “sum rule” and “constant squared” – done. Common error: adding standard deviations ($n\sigma$) instead of variances ($n\sigma^2$). The independence assumption matters: duplicated or clustered observations break $\sigma^2/n$.

Exercise – expected value and variance: warranty claims

An electronics retailer registers $X$ warranty claims per week. From long experience the distribution is known:

claims per week $x$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$P(X=x)$$0.2$$0.4$$0.3$$0.1$
  1. Compute $E(X)$, $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ and the standard deviation $\sigma$.
  2. Each claim costs $150$ euros; on top come fixed handling costs of $100$ euros per week, i.e. $K=100+150\,X$. Find $E(K)$ and the standard deviation of $K$.
  3. Weeks are independent. For a quarter of $n=9$ weeks, find the expected value and the standard deviation of the total $S=X_1+\dots+X_9$ and of the mean $\bar X=S/9$.
Talk track: a five-minute warm-up for the two rules. Part c) is the point of the exercise: the total of $9$ weeks has standard deviation $3\cdot0.9$, not $9\cdot0.9$ – variances add, standard deviations do not – and the mean of $9$ weeks wobbles only a third as much as a single week.

Solution (1/2) – warranty claims: single week and costs

Rules: $E(X)=\sum_i x_i\,P(X=x_i)$;  $\mathrm{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$;  $E(a+bX)=a+b\,E(X)$,  $\mathrm{Var}(a+bX)=b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)$.

\begin{align*} \text{a)}\quad E(X) &= 0\cdot0.2+1\cdot0.4+2\cdot0.3+3\cdot0.1 =\mathbf{1.3}\\ E(X^2) &= 0\cdot0.2+1\cdot0.4+4\cdot0.3+9\cdot0.1=2.5\\ \mathrm{Var}(X) &= 2.5-1.3^2=2.5-1.69=\mathbf{0.81}, \qquad \sigma=\sqrt{0.81}=\mathbf{0.9}\\[4pt] \text{b)}\quad E(K) &= 100+150\cdot1.3=100+195=\mathbf{295\ euros}\\ \mathrm{Var}(K) &= 150^2\cdot0.81=18\,225, \qquad \sigma_K=150\cdot0.9=\mathbf{135\ euros} \end{align*}

On average $1.3$ claims and $295$ euros of warranty costs per week, typically fluctuating by about $\pm135$ euros. The fixed $100$ euros shift the costs but do not spread them.

Solution (2/2) – warranty claims: total and mean of 9 weeks

Rules: expected values always add; for independent weeks the variances add;  $\bar X=S/n$ $\Rightarrow$ $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$.

\begin{align*} \text{c)}\quad E(S) &= 9\cdot1.3=\mathbf{11.7}, & \mathrm{Var}(S) &= 9\cdot0.81=7.29, & \mathrm{sd}(S) &= \sqrt{7.29}=\mathbf{2.7}\\ E(\bar X) &= \mathbf{1.3}, & \mathrm{Var}(\bar X) &= 0.81/9=0.09, & \mathrm{sd}(\bar X) &= 0.9/\sqrt9=\mathbf{0.3} \end{align*}

The total spreads $\sqrt9=3$ times as much as one week – not $9$ times ($9\cdot0.9=8.1$ is the typical mistake: variances add, standard deviations do not). The mean spreads only a third as much; this $\sigma/\sqrt n$ returns below as the standard error.

import numpy as np
x = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3]); p = np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.1])
EX = np.sum(x * p); VarX = np.sum(x ** 2 * p) - EX ** 2
print(round(EX, 2), round(VarX, 2))                               # a)  -> 1.3 0.81
print(round(np.sqrt(9 * VarX), 2), round(np.sqrt(VarX / 9), 2))   # c)  -> 2.7 0.3

The normal distribution $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$

many bottles $\rightarrow$ bell curve
$\mu$ = location, $\sigma$ = spread

Density of $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\;e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \quad x\in\mathbb{R} \]

  • $E(X)=\mu$ (location), $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$ (spread); symmetric bell around $\mu$.
  • Probabilities are areas under $f$.
  • Careful: scipy wants the standard deviation, stats.norm(loc=μ, scale=σ).

Standardisation: one table for all normal distributions

Standard normal distribution

$Z\sim N(0,1)$ has density $\varphi(z)$ and CDF $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$. Normal areas have no closed formula, but every normal variable can be standardised:

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),\qquad\text{conversely } X=\mu+\sigma Z . \]

Milk: a single bottle, $X\sim N(1000,\,3^2)$

\[ P(X\le 997)=P\Bigl(Z\le\tfrac{997-1000}{3}\Bigr)=\Phi(-1) =1-\Phi(1)=1-0.8413=\mathbf{0.1587} \]

$997$ ml lies one standard deviation below $\mu$: about $16\,\%$ of the bottles hold at most $997$ ml.

Standardising reduces every normal probability to one table – that of $\Phi$.

Talk track: “$z$ = distance from the mean in standard deviations” – say it until it sticks. Work the milk example on the board: subtract $\mu$, divide by $\sigma$, look up $\Phi(1)$, use symmetry. Point ahead: the standardised mean (and later the test statistic) is exactly this move applied to $\bar X$, with the standard error instead of $\sigma$.

Exercise – normal distribution: fill volume

A bottling plant fills orange juice into 1-litre bottles. The fill volume $X$ (in ml) is normally distributed with $\mu=1000$ and $\sigma=10$, i.e. $X\sim N(1000,\,10^2)$. The legal minimum content is $985$ ml.

  1. What share of the bottles is underfilled, $P(X<985)$?
  2. With what probability does a bottle contain between $990$ and $1015$ ml?
  3. Which fill volume $x_{0.95}$ is exceeded by only $5\,\%$ of the bottles?
  4. The share of underfilled bottles is to be cut to at most $1\,\%$. To which setpoint $\mu$ must the plant be adjusted if $\sigma=10$ stays the same?

Hint: standardise, $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$, and use the $\Phi$ table.

Talk track: give 5–7 minutes. Insist on a sketch of the bell curve for every part – a) and b) are areas (forwards), c) and d) are quantiles (backwards). Part d) is the business punchline: the safety margin costs juice in every single bottle.

Solution (1/3) – fill volume: probabilities

Rules: $P(X\le x)=\Phi\bigl(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\bigr)$;  $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$;  $P(a\le X\le b)=\Phi(z_b)-\Phi(z_a)$.

\begin{align*} \text{a)}\quad P(X<985) &= \Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{985-1000}{10}\Bigr)=\Phi(-1.5)\\ &=1-\Phi(1.5)=1-0.9332=\mathbf{0.0668}\\[10pt] \text{b)}\quad P(990\le X\le 1015) &=\Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{1015-1000}{10}\Bigr)-\Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{990-1000}{10}\Bigr) =\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(-1)\\ &=0.9332-(1-0.8413)=0.9332-0.1587=\mathbf{0.7745} \end{align*}

Interpretation

About $6.7\,\%$ of the bottles are underfilled; about $77.5\,\%$ contain between $990$ and $1015$ ml.

Solution (2/3) – fill volume: quantile and setpoint

Rules: quantile $x_p=\mu+\sigma z_p$ (search $p$ inside the table);  $z_{1-p}=-z_p$.

\begin{align*} \text{c)}\quad &\Phi(z)=0.95\ \Rightarrow\ z_{0.95}=1.645 \ \Rightarrow\ x_{0.95}=1000+1.645\cdot10=\mathbf{1016.45\ ml}\\[4pt] \text{d)}\quad &P(X<985)=0.01\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac{985-\mu}{10}=z_{0.01}=-z_{0.99}\approx-2.33 \quad(\text{table: }\Phi(2.33)=0.9901)\\ &\Rightarrow\ \mu=985+2.33\cdot10=\mathbf{1008.3\ ml} \quad(\text{exact } z_{0.99}=2.3263:\ 1008.26\text{ ml}) \end{align*}

Estimators are random variables

The sample $X_1,\dots,X_n$ is random, so anything computed from it – $\bar X$, $s$, and later the test statistics – is itself a random variable.

  • Its distribution over all possible samples is the sampling distribution – so $\bar X$ and $s$ each have their own.

The standard error: how much $\bar X$ wobbles

Definition

The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator – the spread of $\bar X$ from sample to sample, not the spread of the raw data.

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\bar X)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\quad \Bigl(\text{or }\tfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\text{ when }\sigma\text{ is estimated}\Bigr). \]

  • It is the natural ruler for inference: we will measure how many standard errors $\bar x$ lies away from a reference value – this standardised quantity reappears as the test statistic in Section 3.
  • Larger $n$ $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE $\Rightarrow$ narrower CI and sharper test.

Standard deviation = spread of the data;    standard error = spread of the estimate.

The central limit theorem – intuition

Our inference rests on $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$. But real data – waiting times, incomes, processing times – are often skewed. Does the recipe still work?

  • Draw $n$ independent values from any distribution (skewed, flat, bimodal) with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.
  • Their mean $\bar X$ is approximately normal once $n$ is large enough – the shape of the original distribution washes out.
  • Why? In an average, unusually high and unusually low values partly cancel out; what remains is a symmetric bell around $\mu$.

Whatever the shape of the data: for large $n$ the sample mean is approximately normal, with centre $\mu$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$.

Talk track: ask first – “waiting times at a checkout are skewed; is the AVERAGE waiting time of 50 customers skewed too?” Let them guess, then show the simulation on the next slide. Keep the distinction sharp: the CLT is about averages (and sums), never about single observations.

The central limit theorem – formal statement

Central limit theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy)

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with $E(X_i)=\mu$ and $0<\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty$. Then

\[ Z_n=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ \xrightarrow{\;d\;}\ N(0,1) \quad (n\to\infty), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \bar X\ \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim}\ N\Bigl(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Bigr). \]

For large $n$ the standardised mean behaves like a standard normal variable – whatever the distribution of the single values.

  • $\xrightarrow{d}$ = convergence in distribution: the CDF of $Z_n$ approaches $\Phi$.
  • Rule of thumb: $n\gtrsim 30$ is usually enough; strongly skewed data need more.
  • For normal data, $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$ holds exactly, for every $n$.
  • The CLT is about $\bar X$ – it does not make the single values normal.
Talk track: connect to what follows – every CI and test in this part standardises $\bar X$ like $Z_n$; the CLT is the licence to do so for non-normal data with $n\gtrsim30$ (assumptions checklist later). Typical exam trap: “by the CLT the data are normal” – no, only the mean is.

A single estimate is not enough

Intuition

From the sample we compute the mean $\bar x$. But it almost never hits the true value $\mu$ exactly – a second sample would give a different value.

  • Instead of a single point we report a range of values that are compatible with the data.
  • The more precise our estimate, the narrower this range.

A confidence interval reports the estimate together with its uncertainty – a range of plausible values for $\mu$. (It is not a probability statement about where $\mu$ lies – see the interpretation slides.)

How we read probability: the frequentist view

Fixed vs. random

  • Parameters $\mu,\sigma$ are fixed (unknown) constants – not random.
  • The data – and all we build from them ($\bar X$, $s$, $Z_n$, the CI bounds, later the test results) – are random.

What “probability” means here

The long-run relative frequency of an event over (hypothetically) very many repeated samples.

“$95\,\%$ confidence” and “$5\,\%$ significance” describe the procedure across repeated samples – not one particular interval and not one particular dataset.

How does the mean fluctuate? – formally

Sampling distribution of the sample mean

For $X_i \sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$ i.i.d., the sampling distribution of the random variable $\bar X$ is

\[ \bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\,\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z_n := \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1). \]

Both results are from the Foundations ($\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)= \sigma^2/n$, standardisation). New is the probability statement below – the building block of the confidence interval.

Two-sided probability statement

\[ P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \le Z_n \le z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha \]

With probability $1-\alpha$, $Z_n$ lies between the two quantiles $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}$.

Derivation (1/2): rearrange step by step

Start from the pivot $Z_n=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)$, which lies within $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}$ with probability $1-\alpha$:

\begin{align*} 1-\alpha &= P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\ \le\ \tfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ \le\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small starting point}\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ \bar X-\mu\ \le\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small multiply all 3 parts by }\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}>0\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(-\bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ -\mu\ \le\ -\bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small subtract }\bar X\text{ everywhere} \end{align*}

Multiplying by a positive number keeps the “$\le$” signs; we only used equivalence steps, so the probability stays exactly $1-\alpha$.

Talk track: the whole derivation is just “solve the inequality for $\mu$”. Announce that upfront so the algebra has a destination. Emphasise at the end of (2/2): the randomness sits in the BOUNDS (they contain $\bar X$), $\mu$ never moves – that is what later justifies the careful CI interpretation.

Derivation (2/2): from $-\mu$ to the interval

\begin{align*} 1-\alpha &= P\!\left(\bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \ge\ \mu\ \ge\ \bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small multiply by }(-1):\ \text{signs flip}\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(\bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ \mu\ \le\ \bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small just read it left-to-right} \end{align*}

Confidence interval with known variance:

\[ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[\bar{X}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Multiplying an inequality by $-1$ reverses “$\le$” into “$\ge$” – that is the only subtle step. What is random are the bounds (they contain $\bar X$); $\mu$ itself is a fixed unknown.

The formula in words

\[ \text{CI} = \big[\;\underbrace{\bar{X}}_{\text{estimate}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{z_{1-\alpha/2}}_{\substack{\text{critical}\\\text{value}}} \cdot \underbrace{\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}_{\text{standard error}}\;\big] \]

  • Centre $\bar X$: our best estimate of $\mu$.
  • Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: how strongly $\bar X$ fluctuates.
  • Critical value $z_{1-\alpha/2}$: how many standard errors we go to the left/right ($1.96$ at $95\,\%$).

Milk example: calculation

Given: $\bar{x}=1001$, $\sigma=3$, $n=9$, $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

\begin{align*} \text{margin of error } e &= z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.96\cdot\frac{3}{\sqrt{9}} = 1.96\\[2pt] \text{lower bound} &= 1001 - 1.96 = 999.04\\ \text{upper bound} &= 1001 + 1.96 = 1002.96 \end{align*}

Result

\[ \text{CI}_{0.95} = \bigl[\,999.04\,;\,1002.96\,\bigr]\,\text{ml} \]

The hypothesised target value $\mu_0 = 1000$ lies inside the interval $\Rightarrow$ no evidence against a correctly working machine.

What does “95 % confidence” mean?

 

Of $20$ computed $95\,\%$ intervals, on average $\approx 19$ cover the true value $\mu$ (red line). The confidence level refers to the procedure across many samples.

Each row: one sample of $n=9$ bottles and its CI. Draw new samples or run 50 rounds – the long-run share settles near the confidence level.

A common misconception

Careful with the interpretation

“With $95\,\%$ probability $\mu$ lies in this particular interval” is, strictly speaking, wrong.

  • $\mu$ is a fixed (unknown) number – it either is or is not inside the particular interval.
  • What is random are the bounds of the interval, not $\mu$.
  • Correct: “The procedure yields, in $95\,\%$ of samples, an interval that covers $\mu$.”

Three correct ways to read a confidence interval

  1. Coverage (the procedure). The method produces intervals that contain the fixed true $\mu$ in a fraction $(1-\alpha)$ of all samples.
  2. Compatibility. The interval is the range of values for $\mu$ that are consistent with the observed data.
  3. Duality with the test. It is exactly the set of null values $\mu_0$ that a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test would not reject.

All three avoid the trap of putting a probability on this one interval: $\mu$ is fixed, the interval is what is random.

Planning the sample size

How many bottles must the dairy measure so that the $95\,\%$ CI is at most $\pm e$ wide – e.g. $\pm1$ ml?

Solve the margin of error for $n$

\[ e=z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sqrt n=\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{e} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\ \ge\ \Bigl(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{e}\Bigr)^{2} \quad\text{(round \emph{up})} \]

  • $n$ grows with the square of $z\sigma/e$: halving the margin of error needs four times as many observations.
  • Higher confidence (larger $z$) or a larger spread $\sigma$ also cost sample size.
  • $\sigma$ unknown? Plan with an estimate from a pilot study or from past data.

Decide on the precision you need before collecting data – the formula tells you what it costs.

More realistic: the variance is unknown

Problem

In practice $\sigma^{2}$ is almost never known. We must also estimate it from the sample.

Estimator of the variance

\[ s^{2} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^{2} \]

  • We replace $\sigma$ by $s$ in the test statistic.
  • This costs accuracy – and changes the distribution (see next slide).

Why the $t$-distribution? – intuitively and graphically

Standardised statistic (normal data)

\[ T_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \]

  • $s$ is itself a random variable.
  • $T_n$ is a ratio of two random quantities $\Rightarrow$ heavier tails.
  • For large $n$, $s$ barely varies, so $t_{n-1}\to N(0,1)$.

Unknown $\sigma$ $\Rightarrow$ use $t_{n-1}$: larger quantiles, so the CI is wider on average.

Milk example with unknown variance

Confidence interval

\[ \text{CI} = \left[\bar{X} - t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X} + t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $n=9$, $\alpha=0.05$, $s = 3.7749$, $t_{0.975;\,8} = 2.3060$.

\begin{align*} e &= 2.3060\cdot\frac{3.7749}{\sqrt{9}} = 2.9017\\ \text{CI}_{0.95} &= [\,1001\pm 2.9017\,] = [\,998.10\,;\,1003.90\,] \end{align*}

What is a hypothesis test? – intuition

The basic idea, as in court

An assumption (the null hypothesis $H_0$) is held true until the data speak strongly enough against it.

  • $H_0$ = “innocent” (status quo).
  • We reject $H_0$ only on convincing evidence.
  • Little evidence $\Rightarrow$ acquittal for lack of evidence – not “proven innocent”.

Milk

$H_0:\mu = 1000$ – “the machine works correctly”. Only a clear deviation from $1000$ makes us doubt this.

The two hypotheses

Structure

\[ H_0:\mu = \mu_0 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1:\mu \neq \mu_0 \]

  • We always argue under “$H_0$ is true”.
  • Why unequal? $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ fixes a single value, so the test statistic has one definite distribution we can compute with; $H_1$ is a whole range, with no single distribution.
  • Two outcomes: keep $H_0$ or reject it.

What you want to establish statistically goes into $H_1$.

Which hypothesis goes where?

Rule of thumb

The statement you want to find evidence for – the “more important” claim – is formulated as $H_1$, because only the Type I error (wrongly rejecting $H_0$) can be controlled via $\alpha$.

You want evidence that …$H_0$$H_1$
the supplier's fill weight deviates from the spec$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$
the mean processing time changed after the update$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$
the mean daily revenue differs from the plan figure$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$

Bridge: confidence interval and test are the same

Testing with the CI

If $\mu_0$ lies inside the confidence interval, $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ is not rejected – if it lies outside, it is.

Equivalence – same algebra, two readings

“$\mu_0$ inside the CI” means $|\bar x-\mu_0|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt n$, which rearranges to $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$ – exactly the non-rejection condition. So a test (level $\alpha$) and a CI (level $1-\alpha$) always agree.

The CI shows all $\mu_0$ that you would not reject.

  • Test reading: blue = acceptance region ($=$ non-rejection region), orange = rejection region ($\alpha$).

Exercise – hypotheses and deciding with the CI

A bank plans its staff with a mean processing time of $\mu_0=30$ min per loan application. Longer times (bottlenecks) and shorter times (overcapacity) matter equally. From experience $\sigma=6$ min is known. A sample of $n=36$ applications gives $\bar x=32.8$ min;  $\alpha=0.05$.

  1. State $H_0$ and $H_1$.
  2. Compute the $95\,\%$ CI for $\mu$.
  3. Decide about $H_0$ with the CI.
  4. Which of the planning values $\mu_0=31$, $33$ and $35$ min would a two-sided test at level $5\,\%$ reject?

Hint: $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

Talk track: four minutes. Part a) practises “the deviation you want to detect goes into $H_1$”; part d) is the payoff of the duality – one interval answers the test for every $\mu_0$ at a glance, without a single new calculation.

Solution – hypotheses and deciding with the CI

Rule: $\mu_0$ inside the $(1-\alpha)$-CI $\Leftrightarrow$ $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ keep $H_0$.

  1. $H_0:\mu=30$  vs.  $H_1:\mu\neq30$ – both directions count; the deviation to be detected goes into $H_1$.
  2. $\mathrm{SE}=6/\sqrt{36}=6/6=1$,  $e=1.96\cdot1=1.96$,  $\text{CI}_{0.95}=[\,32.8\pm1.96\,]=\mathbf{[\,30.84\,;\,34.76\,]}$ min.
  3. $30\notin\text{CI}$ $\Rightarrow$ $H_0$ is rejected: the mean processing time deviates from the planning value. (Cross-check: $|Z_n|=|32.8-30|/1=2.8>1.96$.)
  4. One look at the CI suffices: $31$ and $33$ lie inside $[\,30.84\,;\,34.76\,]$ $\Rightarrow$ keep;  $35$ lies outside $\Rightarrow$ reject.

A CI answers the two-sided test for every hypothesised value $\mu_0$ at once.

The critical value – the bar you must clear

Plain idea

The critical value is a fixed threshold on the test statistic. It splits all possible outcomes into two zones:

Keep $H_0$

Statistic inside the threshold – the deviation is small enough to pass as ordinary chance.

Reject $H_0$

Statistic beyond the threshold – too extreme to be chance at level $\alpha$.

Talk track: “A critical value is just a line in the sand on the test statistic.” Use the high-jump image – $\alpha$ sets the bar height; smaller $\alpha$ = higher bar = harder to reject. Common student confusion: thinking a larger statistic is automatically “better” – it only matters relative to the bar. Anchor: the acceptance region is everything inside the bar(s).

The test recipe (known variance)

1. Hypotheses
$H_0:\mu=\mu_0$
$H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0$
→
2. Test statistic
$Z_n=\dfrac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$
→
3. Critical value
$z_{1-\alpha/2}$
→
$|Z_n|>z_{1-\alpha/2}$?
yes: reject $H_0$
no: keep $H_0$
  • Key move: plug the hypothesised $\mu_0$ in for the unknown $\mu$ (the same $Z_n$ as for the CI, only with $\mu_0$ instead of $\mu$) – if $H_0$ holds then $Z_n\sim N(0,1)$, so a $|Z_n|$ that would be very unusual for $N(0,1)$ is evidence against $H_0$.
  • $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$: acceptance (non-rejection) region; beyond it: rejection region (area $\alpha$ under $H_0$). Fix $\alpha$ in advance.

Milk test with known variance

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $\sigma=3$, $n=9$, $\mu_0=1000$, $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

\begin{align*} Z_n &= \frac{1001-1000}{3/\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{1} = 1.000\\[2pt] |Z_n| &= 1.000 \;<\; 1.96 = z_{0.975} \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\mu=1000$ is not rejected – no evidence against a correctly working machine.

Same result as the CI: $\mu_0=1000 \in [999.04\,;\,1002.96]$.

Test with unknown variance

Only two changes to the recipe

\[ T_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}\ \text{under } H_0, \qquad \text{rejection: } |T_n| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}. \]

  • $\sigma \to s$ (estimated) and $z \to t_{n-1}$.
  • Assumption: data (approximately) normally distributed or $n \ge 30$ (central limit theorem).

Milk test with unknown variance

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $s=3.7749$, $n=9$, $\mu_0=1000$, $\alpha=0.05$, $t_{0.975;\,8}=2.3060$.

\begin{align*} T_n &= \frac{1001-1000}{3.7749/\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{1.2583} = 0.7947\\[2pt] |T_n| &= 0.7947 \;<\; 2.3060 = t_{0.975;\,8} \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\mu=1000$ is not rejected. (More cautious $t$-quantile $2.31$ instead of $1.96$ – the result is nevertheless the same here.)

Before you test – the assumptions checklist

  1. Random sample, independent observations – the $X_i$ are i.i.d. draws from the population. (No clustering, no repeated measurements of the same unit.)
  2. Distribution: data approximately normal or $n\gtrsim 30$ – then the CLT (Foundations) makes $\bar X$ approximately normal.
  3. Spread: $\sigma$ known $\to$ $z$-test;  $\sigma$ estimated by $s$ $\to$ $t$-test with $\mathrm{df}=n-1$.
  4. Question fixed in advance: $\mu_0$, the direction (one-/two-sided) and $\alpha$ are chosen before seeing the results.

If 1.–2. fail, the stated $\alpha$ is not honest: the $5\,\%$ false-alarm guarantee only holds under the model assumptions.

Talk track: a test is a measurement instrument – the checklist is its calibration. Point 1 is the one most often violated in practice (convenience samples, duplicated units). For point 2, with $n=9$ as in the milk example we genuinely rely on approximate normality of fill volumes; mention a histogram or QQ-plot as the quick diagnostic. Point 4 anticipates the one-sided tests (appendix): direction-after-data doubles the true $\alpha$.

The $p$-value – intuition

A single question

“How surprising are my data if $H_0$ were true?”

  • Small $p$-value ($\approx 0$): such an extreme result would be very rare under $H_0$ $\Rightarrow$ doubt about $H_0$.
  • Large $p$-value ($\approx 1$): the data are unsurprising under $H_0$ $\Rightarrow$ no reason to doubt it (but not positive proof of $H_0$).

The $p$-value measures how surprising the data are if $H_0$ holds – the smaller, the stronger the evidence against $H_0$.

The $p$-value – graphically

The (two-sided) $p$-value is the total area in both tails that is at least as extreme as the observed value. Drag the blue handle; switch $H_1$ to see the one‑sided tail.

The $p$-value – definition

Definition

The $p$-value is the probability, under the validity of $H_0$, of observing a test statistic that is at least as extreme as the one actually observed.

  • “At least as extreme” is set by $H_1$: two-sided ($\mu\neq\mu_0$) $=$ both tails $|Z|\ge|Z_n|$; one-sided $=$ only the tail in the $H_1$ direction. Same statistic, different $p$.
  • $0 \le p \le 1$; software (e.g. scipy.stats) always reports it.
  • Advantage: more informative than a bare yes/no – it quantifies the strength of the evidence.
Talk track: read the definition twice, slowly – every word carries weight. “Under the validity of $H_0$” = we compute in the $H_0$ world. “At least as extreme” = the observed value or anything further out, where “out” is defined by $H_1$. Warn explicitly: the $p$-value is NOT the probability that $H_0$ is true – that misreading is the single most common exam error (dedicated slide follows).

Decision rule

One rule for all tests

\[ p < \alpha \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{reject } H_0 \qquad\qquad p \ge \alpha \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{keep } H_0 \]

  • Left: $p$ large, value in the acceptance region.
  • Right: $p$ small, value in the rejection region.
  • $\alpha$ is the “benchmark”.

Drag either handle across the grey critical value; move $\alpha$ to shift the benchmark.

Two rules, one decision: critical value $\leftrightarrow$ $p$-value

  • $\alpha$ = tail area beyond the critical value (fixed in advance).
  • $p$ = tail area beyond your observed statistic.
  • Statistic past the bar $\Leftrightarrow$ its tail is smaller $\Leftrightarrow p<\alpha$.
  • Picture: right-sided test ($z_{0.95}=1.645$); two-sided: both tails, $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

The two decision rules are identical: “test statistic beyond the critical value” and “$p<\alpha$” always agree – the critical value is simply the statistic whose tail area equals $\alpha$.

Both rules, one verdict – worked example

Cream, two-sided, known $\sigma$:   $Z_n=-2.125$,   $\alpha=0.05$.

Critical-value rule

\[ |Z_n| = 2.125 \;>\; 1.96 = z_{0.975} \]

$\Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$.

$p$-value rule

\[ p = 2\,\Phi(-2.125) = 0.034 \]

$0.034 < 0.05 \Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$.

Same data, same $\alpha$, same decision – because $|Z_n|>z_{0.975}$ and $p<\alpha$ are just two ways of saying the observed value sits past the bar.

Exercise – $p$-value and the choice of $\alpha$

A two-sided test with known variance yields $Z_n = 1.85$. Compute the $p$-value and decide at $\alpha = 0.05$ and at $\alpha = 0.10$. Check both decisions with the critical-value rule.

Hint: $\Phi(1.85) = 0.9678$ (table: row $1.8$, column $.05$); two-sided as in the worked example: $p=2\,\Phi(-|Z_n|)$;   $z_{0.975}=1.96$,   $z_{0.95}=1.645$.

Solution – $p$-value and $\alpha$

Rule: two-sided $p=2\,\Phi(-|Z_n|)=2\,\bigl(1-\Phi(|Z_n|)\bigr)$ (symmetry); reject $H_0$ if $p<\alpha$.

\[ p = 2\,\bigl(1-\Phi(1.85)\bigr) = 2\cdot 0.0322 = \mathbf{0.0644} \qquad(\text{exact, unrounded }\Phi\text{: }0.0643) \]

  • At $\alpha = 0.05$:   $0.0644 > 0.05 \Rightarrow$ keep $H_0$.   Check: $|Z_n|=1.85<1.96=z_{0.975}$.
  • At $\alpha = 0.10$:   $0.0644 < 0.10 \Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$.   Check: $|Z_n|=1.85>1.645=z_{0.95}$ (two‑sided at $10\,\%$ uses $z_{1-0.10/2}=z_{0.95}$).

The same data can be “significant” or not – $\alpha$ must be fixed in advance, never chosen to fit the result.

Computing the $p$-value (two-sided)

Known variance

\[ p = 2\cdot\Phi\!\bigl(-|Z_n|\bigr) = P\!\bigl(|Z| > |Z_n|\bigr), \quad Z\sim N(0,1) \]

Unknown variance

\[ p = 2\cdot F_{t,n-1}\!\bigl(-|T_n|\bigr) \]

Why double? With $H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0$ a deviation in either direction counts, so “at least as extreme” means both tails $|Z|>|Z_n|$; by symmetry each has area $\Phi(-|Z_n|)$ – hence the factor $2$. (One-sided: a single tail, no doubling.)

from scipy import stats
milk = np.array([1005, 1001, 997, 1000, 1001, 998, 999, 999, 1009])

    n = len(x)
    z = (x.mean() - mu0) / (sigma / np.sqrt(n))           # sigma known
    t = (x.mean() - mu0) / (x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n))   # sigma estimated
    p_z = 2 * stats.norm.sf(abs(z))                       # 2 * (1 - Phi(|z|))
    p_t = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t), df=n - 1)                # 2 * (1 - F_t,n-1(|t|))
    print(f"z = {z:.4f}, p = {p_z:.4f} | t = {t:.4f}, p = {p_t:.4f}")

$p$-value – typical misconceptions

What the $p$-value is not

  • Not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
  • A large $p$-value does not prove $H_0$ – it only shows no contradiction.

Correct – mind the direction

The $p$-value is the probability of the data (or more extreme) assuming $H_0$ (chance) holds: $P(\text{data}\mid H_0)$. It conditions on chance; it does not give the probability that chance was the cause, $P(H_0\mid\text{data})$.

Talk track: use the courtroom again – $P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{innocent})$ is not $P(\text{innocent}\mid\text{evidence})$. Concrete anchor: “the probability that a randomly tested person has these symptoms IF healthy” vs. “the probability the person is healthy GIVEN the symptoms” – clearly different. Also stress the second bullet: a large $p$ means the data cannot distinguish $H_0$ from nearby alternatives – absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

Significant $\neq$ important – the large-$n$ trap

The $p$-value measures evidence, not size

With enough data, any tiny deviation from $\mu_0$ becomes statistically significant – even one that is practically irrelevant.

Milk machine, observed $\bar x = 1000.2$ ml ($\mu_0=1000$, $\sigma=3$)

$Z_n$$p$ (two-sided)verdict at $\alpha=0.05$
$n=9$$0.20$$0.84$not significant

The same irrelevant $0.2$ ml deviation – invisible at $n=9$, “highly significant” at $n=10\,000$. So report the effect size and the CI, not just $p$.

Talk track: significance answers “is there evidence of ANY deviation?”, not “is the deviation large enough to matter?”. With $n=10\,000$ the SE is $0.03$ ml – the test becomes a microscope. Flip side (links to power): with small $n$, even an important deviation often stays invisible. Practical rule for the students: $p$-value for evidence, CI for magnitude – always read both.

Two ways to be wrong

Intuition

Like a smoke detector, we can be wrong in two ways: false alarm or missed fire.

Decision$H_0$ true$H_0$ false
reject $H_0$Type I error ($\alpha$)correct (power $1-\beta$)
keep $H_0$correct ($1-\alpha$)Type II error ($\beta$)

Type I error = false alarm (reject although $H_0$ is true).    Type II error = miss (keep although $H_1$ is true).

Talk track: build the table column by column – “first fix the TRUE state of the world (column), then look at what our test DID (row).” Smoke detector: too sensitive = many false alarms (Type I); too sluggish = missed fire (Type II). Make students say which error the milk test could have made (it kept $H_0$, so only a Type II is possible). Common confusion: thinking both errors can occur in the same test run – only one column is ever real.

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ in a picture

  • The two curves show the standardised $\bar X$ in each “world”: left centred at $\mu_0$ (value $0$, if $H_0$ holds), right at the true $\mu_1$ (here $2.6$ standard errors above $\mu_0$, if $H_1$); the red line is the critical value of a right-sided test ($1.645$).
  • $\alpha$: $H_0$ true, but beyond the critical value.
  • $\beta$: $H_1$ true, but on this side.
  • The distributions overlap – both errors cannot be $0$ at once.

Sliders: a smaller $\alpha$ moves the red line right ($\beta$ grows); a larger effect or a larger $n$ pulls the $H_1$ curve away ($\beta$ shrinks).

Type I error ($\alpha$) – controllable

Definition

\[ P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true}) = \alpha \]

  • Directly controllable through the choice of $\alpha$ (typically $0.05$).
  • In $5\,\%$ of cases a true $H_0$ is wrongly rejected.

Milk

$H_0:\mu=1000$ is true, yet the test rejects (false alarm) – probability $=\alpha$.

Type II error ($\beta$) – only indirectly influenced

Definition (at a specific alternative $\mu_1$)

\[ P(\text{keep } H_0 \mid \mu = \mu_1) = \beta \]

  • No direct lever; $\beta$ depends on:
    • $\alpha$, sample size $n$, spread $\sigma^2$,
    • true deviation $|\mu-\mu_0|$ (effect size).
  • At a fixed $\mu_1$: smaller $\alpha$ $\Rightarrow$ higher bar $\Rightarrow$ more misses ($\beta$ rises);   larger $n$ $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE $\Rightarrow$ $\beta$ falls.

Power $=1-\beta$: the ability to actually reject a false $H_0$.

Only one error is controllable

Question: Which error can we only make when we reject $H_0$?

Answer

The Type I error – and that is exactly the one we control via $\alpha$.

Consequence for the choice of hypotheses

This is why the claim you want to establish goes into $H_1$ (rule of thumb from above): wrongly declaring it “established” – rejecting $H_0$ although $H_0$ holds – is the costly mistake, and exactly this one is the controllable Type I error.

Summary 1 – foundations

Normal distribution

$X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$   $\Rightarrow$   $Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)$

$P(X\le x)=\Phi\bigl(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\bigr)$,    $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$

quantile: $x_p=\mu+\sigma\,z_p$,    $z_{0.975}=1.96$

Sampling distribution of $\bar X$

$E(\bar X)=\mu$,    $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$,    $\mathrm{SE}=\sigma/\sqrt n$

normal data: $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$ exactly;

otherwise (CLT, $n\gtrsim30$): approximately.

Every interval and test in this part standardises $\bar X$ with its standard error – and the CLT makes this work even for non-normal data.

Summary 2 – confidence intervals

Known variance $\sigma$    ($Z_n\sim N(0,1)$)

\[ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[\bar{X}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Unknown variance    ($T_n\sim t_{n-1}$)

\[ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[\bar{X}-t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}+t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Sample size for a margin of error $e$ (known $\sigma$):   $n\ge\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/e\bigr)^2$, rounded up.

Summary 3 – hypothesis test (two-sided)

$H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ vs. $H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0$

Test statistics

\[ Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\sim N(0,1),\qquad T_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}\sim t_{n-1} \qquad(\text{under } H_0) \]

Decision

Reject if $|Z_n|>z_{1-\alpha/2}$ ($\sigma$ known) or $|T_n|>t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}$ ($\sigma$ unknown).

Summary 4 – the $p$-value

One idea behind every formula

$p$ = probability, if $H_0$ were true, of a value at least as extreme as observed – in the direction(s) that $H_1$ allows.

Properties of estimators – the formal criteria

An estimator $\hat\theta=\hat\theta(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is a random variable; its value for the observed sample is the estimate.

PropertyFormal definition
Unbiasedness$E(\hat\theta)=\theta$
Bias$\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta)=E(\hat\theta)-\theta$
Asymptotic unbiasedness$\lim_{n\to\infty}E(\hat\theta_n)=\theta$
Efficiency$\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)<\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta^{*})$    (both unbiased)
Consistency$\hat\theta_n\xrightarrow{P}\theta$, i.e. $\lim_{n\to\infty}P\bigl(|\hat\theta_n-\theta|>c\bigr)=0$ for every $c>0$
sufficient for it$\lim_{n\to\infty}E(\hat\theta_n)=\theta$   and   $\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta_n)=0$

unbiased $=$ right on average; efficient $=$ small spread; consistent $=$ arbitrarily precise as $n$ grows. The sample mean $\bar X$ is unbiased and consistent, and the most efficient of all unbiased weighted averages $\sum a_iX_i$.

Why divide by $n-1$ in $s^2$?

Sample variance – unbiased

\[ s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(X_i-\bar X\bigr)^2, \qquad E(s^2)=\sigma^2 . \]

  • We measure the spread around the estimated mean $\bar X$, which sits in the middle of the data – so the squared deviations come out systematically too small.
  • Dividing by $n$ would give $E\bigl(\tfrac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\bigr)=\tfrac{n-1}{n}\,\sigma^2$ – biased downwards; dividing by $n-1$ corrects exactly this.
  • Estimating $\mu$ “uses up” one degree of freedom – the same $n-1$ as in $t_{n-1}$.

Simulation: $100\,000$ samples of size $n=4$ from $N(50,\,5^2)$, i.e. $\sigma^2=25$

Average of $s^2$ with $n-1$: $\mathbf{24.97}$    vs.    with $n$: $\mathbf{18.73}$   ($\approx\tfrac34\cdot25=18.75$).

The law of large numbers – intuition

Die example: a fair die has $E(X)=3.5$. After a few rolls the running mean $\bar x_n$ still jumps around; after many rolls it settles down at $3.5$.

The sample mean $\bar X_n$ gets ever more reliably close to the expected value as $n$ grows.

Twelve sequences of die rolls (grey), one highlighted (blue). The funnel $\mu\pm1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n$ narrows: $\pm1.06$ at $n=10$, $\pm0.33$ at $n=100$, $\pm0.11$ at $n=1000$.

The law of large numbers – formal statement

Weak law of large numbers

For i.i.d. $X_i$ with $E(X_i)=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty$:

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}P\bigl(|\bar X_n-\mu|>c\bigr)=0 \quad\text{for every } c>0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad \bar X_n\xrightarrow{\;P\;}\mu . \]

Choose any tolerance $c$, however small: the probability that $\bar X_n$ misses $\mu$ by more than $c$ goes to zero.

Why? Chebyshev's inequality: $P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>c)\le\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)/c^2=\sigma^2/(n\,c^2)\to0$.

Special case: relative frequencies (Bernoulli)

$X_i=1$ if an event occurs (probability $p$), else $0$: then $\bar X_n$ is the event's relative frequency, and $\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}p$ – the basis of the frequentist view used throughout this part.

LLN: $\bar X_n$ approaches $\mu$.   CLT: how the remaining fluctuation around $\mu$ is distributed.

When only one direction matters

The idea

In a two-sided test any departure from $\mu_0$ counts against $H_0$. Often, though, a deviation is a problem in only one direction – the other direction is harmless or even welcome.

Too little in the bottle

For consumer protection only underfilling ($\mu<1000$) is critical – overfilling does not harm the customer.

Unemployment insurance

Only if the rate exceeds a threshold do contributions fall short. A lower rate causes no problem.

The question – not the data – decides the direction. The deviation we truly care about defines $H_1$, and the whole $\alpha$ goes on that one side instead of $\alpha/2$ on each.

How to find the critical value – 3 steps

  1. Direction (read from $H_1$): one-sided or two-sided?
  2. Tail probability:

  3. Which table? $\sigma$ known $\to z$ (normal); $\sigma$ unknown $\to t$ with $\mathrm{df}=n-1$.

The golden rule of one-sided tests

If you remember only one sentence

We reject $H_0$ when the sample result is sufficiently extreme in the direction specified by the alternative hypothesis $H_1$.

$H_1:\mu<\mu_0$ (smaller) reject if $z$ very negative $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$ (larger) reject if $z$ very positive $H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0$ (different) reject if $|z|$ very large (either way)

The inequality in $H_1$ points straight at the rejection region (orange): “$<$” $\to$ left tail, “$>$” $\to$ right tail, “$\neq$” $\to$ both.

Why put the whole $\alpha$ on one side?

two-sided: $\alpha/2$ each side
one-sided (left): full $\alpha$
  • Two-sided spends $\alpha/2$ guarding each tail – guarding the harmless side is wasted.
  • One-sided puts the whole $\alpha$ in the relevant tail, so the critical value moves inward ($1.96\to1.645$).
  • A true deviation in that direction now clears the bar more easily.

Same $\alpha$, more power – but only in the chosen direction. A one-sided test at level $\alpha$ uses the critical value of a two‑sided test at level $2\alpha$ on that side.

Key sentence to say out loud: “a one-sided test at $\alpha$ uses the same cutoff as a two-sided test at $2\alpha$ on that side” – that is why $1.96$ drops to $1.645$. Frame it as a trade: more power one way, in exchange for zero ability to detect the other way.

The price: you must commit in advance

The direction is part of the hypothesis

Choose one- vs. two-sided before looking at the data – based on the research question alone.

  • Picking the side after seeing which way the data point inflates the true Type I error from $\alpha$ to as much as $2\alpha$.
  • A one-sided test is blind to deviations in the other direction: even a large effect on the “wrong” side never rejects $H_0$.
  • If both directions genuinely matter, use a two-sided test.

One-sided tests buy power with a promise – that the other direction really does not matter.

Left-sided test – consumer protection

Task

A new filling line fills milk (label $1\,\text{l}$). Are the consumer protection requirements met, i.e. is not too little being filled? Test with $1-\alpha=0.95$.

  • $H_0:\mu = 1000$    vs.    $H_1:\mu < 1000$.
  • Only $\mu < 1000$ (too little in the bottle) is critical.

Procedure

Test statistic $T_n = (\bar X - \mu_0)\big/(s/\sqrt n)$; reject if $T_n < -t_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$   (known: $Z_n < -z_{1-\alpha}$).

$p$-value: $p = F_{t,n-1}(T_n)$.

We reject only if $\bar X$ falls clearly below $\mu_0$ – a value above $\mu_0$ can never be evidence for “too little”.

Right-sided test – do not exceed a limit

Task

Advertisement: “Our milk contains at most $1\,\text{mg} = 1000\,\mu\text{g}$ of substance K per litre.” Is this met? We measure in $\mu$g, so $\mu_0 = 1000\,\mu$g.

  • $H_0:\mu = 1000\,\mu\text{g}$    vs.    $H_1:\mu > 1000\,\mu\text{g}$.
  • Exceeding the limit is critical.

Procedure

Reject if $T_n > t_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$   (known: $Z_n > z_{1-\alpha}$).

$p$-value: $p = 1 - F_{t,n-1}(T_n)$.

Mirror image of the left-sided case: we reject only for values clearly above $\mu_0$.

Right-sided test – substance K with numbers

Data (known variance): $\sigma=90\,\mu$g, $n=9$, $\bar x = 1060\,\mu$g, $\mu_0=1000$, $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.95}=1.645$.

One-sided $p$-value – mind the sign

Only the $H_1$-tail counts

The one-sided $p$-value is the probability, under $H_0$, of a value at least as far in the $H_1$ direction as the one observed. The opposite tail is irrelevant – a deviation there supports $H_0$.

Sign check – a big $|z|$ in the wrong direction is not significant

Right-sided test with $z=-2.5$: the statistic is on the $H_0$ side, so $p = 1-\Phi(-2.5) = 0.994 > 0.5$ – you never reject, even though $|z|$ is large. Check the data point in the $H_1$ direction before computing.

Link to two-sided: if the deviation is in the $H_1$ direction, $p_{\text{one}} = p_{\text{two}}/2$; otherwise $p_{\text{one}} = 1-p_{\text{two}}/2$.

The classic trap: a right-sided test with a negative $T_n$. Say: “if your statistic is on the $H_0$ side, $p>0.5$ – stop, you can never reject.” Always check the statistic points the $H_1$ way before computing the tail area.

Exercise – muesli filling (exam-style, one-sided $t$-test)

A plant fills muesli into packs with target weight $\mu_0=250$ g. Overfilling costs money, so management asks: does the plant fill more than $250$ g on average? After maintenance, a sample of $n=25$ packs gives $\bar x=252.6$ g and $s=5.0$ g; $\alpha=0.05$.

Table values: $t_{0.95;\,24}=1.7109$, $t_{0.975;\,24}=2.0639$, $t_{0.99;\,24}=2.4922$, $t_{0.995;\,24}=2.7969$;   $\Phi(2.60)=0.9953$.

  1. State the hypotheses.
  2. Compute the standard error, the test statistic and the critical value, and decide.
  3. Which Python command performs this test for a data vector x?
  4. Bracket the $p$-value with the $t$-table, and approximate it with the normal distribution.
  5. Compute the (two-sided) $95\,\%$ CI for $\mu$.
  6. Decide without new calculation whether $H_0:\mu=253$ is rejected by a two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$.

Solution (1/3) – muesli: hypotheses and test

a) The claim to be established (overfilling) goes into $H_1$ $\Rightarrow$ right-sided:

\[ H_0:\mu=250 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1:\mu>250 \]

b) $\sigma$ unknown $\Rightarrow$ $t$-test with df $=n-1=24$:

\[ \frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\frac{5.0}{\sqrt{25}}=\mathbf{1.0\ g},\qquad T_n=\frac{252.6-250}{1.0}=\mathbf{2.60},\qquad \text{critical value } t_{0.95;\,24}=\mathbf{1.7109} \]

One-sided: the whole $\alpha$ sits in the right tail, so the $0.95$-quantile (not $0.975$).

Decision

$T_n=2.60>1.7109$ $\Rightarrow$ $H_0$ is rejected: the plant fills significantly more than $250$ g on average – it should be readjusted.

Solution (2/3) – muesli: Python and the $p$-value

c) and the exact $p$-value from the summary figures:

from scipy import stats
# raw data x available:  stats.ttest_1samp(x, popmean=250, alternative="greater")
t = (252.6 - 250) / (5.0 / 25 ** 0.5)                  # from the summary figures
print(f"{t:.2f} {stats.t.sf(t, df=24):.4f} {stats.norm.sf(t):.4f}")
    
2.60 0.0079 0.0047

d) $T_n$ lies between the $0.99$- and the $0.995$-quantile:

\[ t_{0.99;\,24}=2.4922 \;<\; 2.60 \;<\; 2.7969=t_{0.995;\,24} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{0.005<p<0.01} \]

One-sided: the upper-tail areas $0.01$ and $0.005$ are the bounds – no doubling (exact: $p=0.0079$).

Normal approximation: $p\approx1-\Phi(2.60)=1-0.9953=\mathbf{0.0047}$ – smaller than the exact $0.0079$, because the $t$-distribution has heavier tails. Both are far below $0.05$: same decision.

Solution (3/3) – muesli: CI and duality

\[ \textbf{e)}\quad 252.6\pm2.0639\cdot1.0=252.6\pm2.0639 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{CI}_{0.95}=\mathbf{[\,250.54\,;\,254.66\,]}\ \text{g} \]

f) The two-sided CI contains exactly the values $\mu_0$ that a two-sided test does not reject:

\[ 250.54<253<254.66 \ \Rightarrow\ H_0:\mu=253 \text{ is \textbf{not} rejected.} \]

Every $\mu_0<250.54$ or $\mu_0>254.66$ would be rejected – e.g. $\mu_0=250$.

Careful with the duality

The two-sided CI belongs to the two-sided test. The partner of the right-sided test in b) is the one-sided CI $[\,252.6-1.7109\cdot1.0\,;\,\infty)=[\,250.89\,;\,\infty)$ – it excludes $250$, consistent with the rejection.

Talk track: typical exam errors in this task – $t_{0.975}$ instead of $t_{0.95}$ for a one-sided test; doubling the one-sided $p$-value; swapping the bracket ($t_{0.99}$ belongs to a tail area of $0.01$, not $0.99$); df $=25$ instead of $24$. The normal approximation shows nicely why the $t$-tails are heavier.

Exercise – delivery time revisited (one-sided test vs. two-sided CI)

The logistics provider from the delivery-time exercise now advertises a mean delivery time below $\mu_0=48$ hours. A new sample of $n=16$ shipments gives $\bar x=46.4$ h and $s=3.2$ h; $\alpha=0.05$.

Table values: $t_{0.95;\,15}=1.7531$, $t_{0.975;\,15}=2.1314$.

  1. State the hypotheses and carry out the test with the critical value.
  2. Compute the two-sided $95\,\%$ CI. Does it contain $48$? Is that a contradiction to a)?
  3. Which confidence interval matches the test in a)? Compute it.
  4. Check a) and c) with Python.

Solution (1/2) – delivery time revisited: left-sided test

a) The advertised claim goes into $H_1$ $\Rightarrow$ left-sided:

\[ H_0:\mu=48 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1:\mu<48 \]

\[ \frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\frac{3.2}{\sqrt{16}}=0.8,\qquad T_n=\frac{46.4-48}{0.8}=\mathbf{-2.00},\qquad \text{critical value: }{-t_{0.95;\,15}}=\mathbf{-1.7531} \]

Decision

$T_n=-2.00<-1.7531$ $\Rightarrow$ $H_0$ is rejected ($p=F_{t,15}(-2.00)=0.0320$): the mean delivery time is significantly below $48$ h – the advertisement is statistically supported.

The negative sign belongs to the result: the rejection region lies on the left. Comparing absolute values would lose the direction.

Solution (2/2) – delivery time revisited: which CI?

b) $46.4\pm2.1314\cdot0.8=46.4\pm1.7051$ $\Rightarrow$ $\text{CI}_{0.95}=\mathbf{[\,44.69\,;\,48.11\,]}$ – it contains $48$! No contradiction: the two-sided CI belongs to the two-sided test, which would indeed keep $H_0$ here ($p=0.064$). The one-sided test puts the whole $\alpha$ on one side and is sharper in that direction.

c) The partner of the left-sided test is the one-sided CI with an upper bound:

\[ \Bigl(-\infty\,;\ 46.4+1.7531\cdot0.8\Bigr]=\mathbf{(-\infty\,;\,47.80]} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 48\ \text{lies outside -- consistent with a).} \]

d)

from scipy import stats
t = (46.4 - 48) / (3.2 / 16 ** 0.5)
print(f"{t:.2f} {stats.t.cdf(t, df=15):.4f}")           # T_n, left-sided p-value
print(f"{46.4 + stats.t.ppf(0.95, df=15) * 0.8:.2f}")   # upper bound of one-sided CI
    
-2.00 0.0320
47.80

With raw data: stats.ttest_1samp(x, 48, alternative="less") and .confidence_interval(0.95).

Talk track: the punchline of this task is the duality rule “like with like”: one-sided test $\leftrightarrow$ one-sided CI, two-sided test $\leftrightarrow$ two-sided CI. Ask the room before showing b): “the test rejected 48 – will the 95 % CI exclude 48?” Many say yes. It is the compact version of the earlier “one- vs. two-sided on the same data” exercise.

One-sided tests at a glance

One-sided tests (unknown variance)

left:   $H_1:\mu<\mu_0$reject if $T_n<-t_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$
right: $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$reject if $T_n> t_{1-\alpha,\,n-1}$

Fix the direction before looking at the data – the whole $\alpha$ then sits on one side.

Trade-off and power

  • $\alpha\!\downarrow \Rightarrow \beta\!\uparrow$ (trade-off).
  • Larger $n$ raises power at every $\mu\neq\mu_0$ (at $\mu_0$ it stays at $\alpha$) – more power at the same level.
  • Power rises with the true deviation.

Drag the grey handle on the deviation axis; at $2$ SE (milk, $n=9$ vs. $36$) the curves give $0.516$ and $0.979$. Lower $\alpha$ and both curves drop.

Practice

Typically $\alpha=0.05$ and desired power $1-\beta\ge 0.80$. A power analysis provides the required $n$.

Computing $\beta$ concretely – milk

Setup: $H_0:\mu=1000$ vs. $H_1:\mu\neq1000$, $\sigma=3$, $n=9$ ($\mathrm{SE}=1$), $\alpha=0.05$.

Step 1 – turn “$|Z_n|\le1.96$” into bounds on $\bar X$: $\mu_0\pm1.96\cdot\mathrm{SE}=1000\pm1.96=[998.04\,;\,1001.96]$.

Step 2 – the truth is $\mu_1=1002$; find how often $\bar X$ still lands in that region, standardising with $\mu_1$:

\begin{align*} \beta &= P\bigl(998.04 \le \bar X \le 1001.96 \;\big|\; \mu=1002\bigr)\\ &= \Phi\!\bigl(\tfrac{1001.96-1002}{1}\bigr) - \Phi\!\bigl(\tfrac{998.04-1002}{1}\bigr) = 0.484 \end{align*}

Result

Power $=1-\beta = 0.516$: a true deviation of $2$ ml is detected with $n=9$ in only just over half the cases.

Talk track: the key move is the change of world. Step 1 lives in the $H_0$ world (where the acceptance region was built); step 2 asks how often $\bar X$ lands in that same region when the TRUTH is $\mu_1=1002$ – so we standardise with $1002$, not $1000$. Students often reuse $\mu_0$ in step 2; catch that explicitly. The sobering message: with $n=9$, a real 2 ml error escapes detection almost half the time.

Four levers for more power

More sample size helps decisively. Same situation with $n=36$ instead of $9$:

\[ \text{SE}=\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{36}}=0.5 \;\Rightarrow\; \beta = 0.021,\quad \text{power}=0.979. \]

Levers of power $1-\beta$

  1. Sample size $n\uparrow$ (strongest lever).
  2. Effect size $|\mu_1-\mu_0|$ (given).
  3. Spread $\sigma\downarrow$ (e.g. measure more precisely).
  4. Level $\alpha\uparrow$ (buys more Type I error).

Quadrupling $n$ from $9$ to $36$ raises the power from $0.52$ to $0.98$.

Talk track: go through the four levers and ask for each “who controls this?” – $n$: the researcher (budget); effect size: nature/the question; $\sigma$: partly the lab (better instruments); $\alpha$: the convention, and raising it is usually unacceptable. So in practice power planning $=$ sample size planning. Connect back to the CI length slide: same logic, $1/\sqrt n$.

$\beta$ and power with Python

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
for n in (9, 36):                        # milk: mu0 = 1000, true mu1 = 1002, sigma = 3
    se = 3 / np.sqrt(n)
    lo, hi = 1000 - 1.96 * se, 1000 + 1.96 * se         # acceptance region (H0 world)
    beta = stats.norm.cdf(hi, 1002, se) - stats.norm.cdf(lo, 1002, se)  # mu1 world
    print(f"n = {n}: beta = {beta:.3f}, power = {1 - beta:.3f}")
pw = NormalIndPower()                    # power of the z-test; ratio=0: one sample
pw.power(effect_size=2/3, nobs1=9, alpha=0.05, ratio=0)              # 0.516
pw.solve_power(effect_size=2/3, power=0.80, alpha=0.05, ratio=0)     # 17.66 -> n = 18

Output

n = 9: beta = 0.484, power = 0.516
n = 36: beta = 0.021, power = 0.979

Effect size $d=|\mu_1-\mu_0|/\sigma=2/3$. solve_power does the power analysis: $80\,\%$ power needs at least $n=18$ bottles.

Exercise – compute $\beta$ and power (two-sided)

Test $H_0:\mu = 100$ vs. $H_1:\mu\neq 100$, known $\sigma = 10$, $n=25$, $\alpha=0.05$. The true mean is actually $\mu_1 = 105$. Find the Type II error $\beta$ and the power.

Step 1: the acceptance region for $\bar X$ under $H_0$.

Solution – $\beta$ and power (two-sided)

$\text{SE} = 10/\sqrt{25} = 2$. Acceptance region: $100 \pm 1.96\cdot 2 = [96.08\,;\,103.92]$.

\begin{align*} \beta &= P(96.08 \le \bar X \le 103.92 \mid \mu = 105)\\ &= \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{103.92-105}{2}\Big) - \Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{96.08-105}{2}\Big) = \Phi(-0.54) - \Phi(-4.46)\\ &= 0.2946 - 0.0000 = \mathbf{0.2946} \end{align*}

Power

$1-\beta = \mathbf{0.7054}$: a true shift of $5$ units is detected in about $71\,\%$ of samples.

Exercise – power of a one-sided test

Now use a right-sided test, $H_1:\mu>100$, with $\sigma=10$, $n=25$, $\alpha=0.05$, true $\mu_1=105$. Find the power and compare with the two-sided value $0.7054$.

Solution – one-sided power

$\text{SE}=2$. Critical mean: $\bar x_{\text{crit}} = 100 + 1.645\cdot 2 = 103.29$.

\[ 1-\beta = P(\bar X > 103.29 \mid \mu=105) = 1-\Phi\!\Big(\tfrac{103.29-105}{2}\Big) = 1-\Phi(-0.855) = \mathbf{0.8037} \]

Comparison

Power $0.804$ vs. $0.705$ two-sided – the one-sided test is more powerful for the same $n$ and $\alpha$, because the whole $\alpha$ sits on the relevant side.
