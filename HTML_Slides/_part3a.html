Random variables, expected value and variance

Random variable

A random variable $X$ assigns a number to every outcome of a random experiment (e.g. the fill volume of the next bottle). Its distribution says which values occur with which probability.

Expected value – probability-weighted mean:

\[ \mu=E(X)=\sum_i x_i\,P(X=x_i) \]

Variance – mean squared deviation from $\mu$:

\[ \sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(X)=E\bigl[(X-\mu)^2\bigr]=E(X^2)-\mu^2 \]

Continuous $X$ with density $f$: sums become integrals, e.g. $E(X)=\int x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

Complaints per day in an online shop

$x$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$P(X=x)$$0.1$$0.3$$0.4$$0.2$

$E(X)=0\cdot0.1+1\cdot0.3+2\cdot0.4+3\cdot0.2=\mathbf{1.7}$

$E(X^2)=0+0.3+1.6+1.8=3.7$

$\mathrm{Var}(X)=3.7-1.7^2=\mathbf{0.81}$,  $\sigma=\mathbf{0.9}$

In the long run: $1.7$ complaints per day on average – a value that no single day can have.

Two rules that explain why averaging helps

Linear transformation

\begin{align*} E(a+bX)&=a+b\,E(X)\\ \mathrm{Var}(a+bX)&=b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X) \end{align*}

Sums

\begin{align*} E(X+Y)&=E(X)+E(Y) && \text{\scriptsize always}\\ \mathrm{Var}(X+Y)&=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y) && \text{\scriptsize if independent} \end{align*}

Applied to the sample mean of i.i.d. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ with $E(X_i)=\mu$, $\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2$:

\[ E(\bar X)=\frac1n\cdot n\mu=\mu,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

$\bar X$ is right on average, and its variance shrinks like $1/n$: the variances of the $n$ values add up to $n\sigma^2$, but averaging divides by $n^2$. (Variances add – standard deviations do not.)

Talk track: two lines of algebra carry the whole part. Write $\bar X=\tfrac1n(X_1+\dots+X_n)$ on the board, apply “sum rule” and “constant squared” – done. Common error: adding standard deviations ($n\sigma$) instead of variances ($n\sigma^2$). The independence assumption matters: duplicated or clustered observations break $\sigma^2/n$.

Exercise – expected value and variance: warranty claims

An electronics retailer registers $X$ warranty claims per week. From long experience the distribution is known:

claims per week $x$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$P(X=x)$$0.2$$0.4$$0.3$$0.1$
  1. Compute $E(X)$, $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ and the standard deviation $\sigma$.
  2. Each claim costs $150$ euros; on top come fixed handling costs of $100$ euros per week, i.e. $K=100+150\,X$. Find $E(K)$ and the standard deviation of $K$.
  3. Weeks are independent. For a quarter of $n=9$ weeks, find the expected value and the standard deviation of the total $S=X_1+\dots+X_9$ and of the mean $\bar X=S/9$.
Talk track: a five-minute warm-up for the two rules. Part c) is the point of the exercise: the total of $9$ weeks has standard deviation $3\cdot0.9$, not $9\cdot0.9$ – variances add, standard deviations do not – and the mean of $9$ weeks wobbles only a third as much as a single week.

Solution (1/2) – warranty claims: single week and costs

Rules: $E(X)=\sum_i x_i\,P(X=x_i)$;  $\mathrm{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$;  $E(a+bX)=a+b\,E(X)$,  $\mathrm{Var}(a+bX)=b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X)$.

\begin{align*} \text{a)}\quad E(X) &= 0\cdot0.2+1\cdot0.4+2\cdot0.3+3\cdot0.1 =\mathbf{1.3}\\ E(X^2) &= 0\cdot0.2+1\cdot0.4+4\cdot0.3+9\cdot0.1=2.5\\ \mathrm{Var}(X) &= 2.5-1.3^2=2.5-1.69=\mathbf{0.81}, \qquad \sigma=\sqrt{0.81}=\mathbf{0.9}\\[4pt] \text{b)}\quad E(K) &= 100+150\cdot1.3=100+195=\mathbf{295\ euros}\\ \mathrm{Var}(K) &= 150^2\cdot0.81=18\,225, \qquad \sigma_K=150\cdot0.9=\mathbf{135\ euros} \end{align*}

On average $1.3$ claims and $295$ euros of warranty costs per week, typically fluctuating by about $\pm135$ euros. The fixed $100$ euros shift the costs but do not spread them.

Solution (2/2) – warranty claims: total and mean of 9 weeks

Rules: expected values always add; for independent weeks the variances add;  $\bar X=S/n$ $\Rightarrow$ $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$.

\begin{align*} \text{c)}\quad E(S) &= 9\cdot1.3=\mathbf{11.7}, & \mathrm{Var}(S) &= 9\cdot0.81=7.29, & \mathrm{sd}(S) &= \sqrt{7.29}=\mathbf{2.7}\\ E(\bar X) &= \mathbf{1.3}, & \mathrm{Var}(\bar X) &= 0.81/9=0.09, & \mathrm{sd}(\bar X) &= 0.9/\sqrt9=\mathbf{0.3} \end{align*}

The total spreads $\sqrt9=3$ times as much as one week – not $9$ times ($9\cdot0.9=8.1$ is the typical mistake: variances add, standard deviations do not). The mean spreads only a third as much; this $\sigma/\sqrt n$ returns below as the standard error.

import numpy as np
x = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3]); p = np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.1])
EX = np.sum(x * p); VarX = np.sum(x ** 2 * p) - EX ** 2
print(round(EX, 2), round(VarX, 2))                               # a)  -> 1.3 0.81
print(round(np.sqrt(9 * VarX), 2), round(np.sqrt(VarX / 9), 2))   # c)  -> 2.7 0.3

The normal distribution $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$

many bottles $\rightarrow$ bell curve
$\mu$ = location, $\sigma$ = spread

Density of $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\;e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \quad x\in\mathbb{R} \]

  • $E(X)=\mu$ (location), $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$ (spread); symmetric bell around $\mu$.
  • Probabilities are areas under $f$.
  • Careful: scipy wants the standard deviation, stats.norm(loc=μ, scale=σ).

Standardisation: one table for all normal distributions

Standard normal distribution

$Z\sim N(0,1)$ has density $\varphi(z)$ and CDF $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$. Normal areas have no closed formula, but every normal variable can be standardised:

\[ X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),\qquad\text{conversely } X=\mu+\sigma Z . \]

Milk: a single bottle, $X\sim N(1000,\,3^2)$

\[ P(X\le 997)=P\Bigl(Z\le\tfrac{997-1000}{3}\Bigr)=\Phi(-1) =1-\Phi(1)=1-0.8413=\mathbf{0.1587} \]

$997$ ml lies one standard deviation below $\mu$: about $16\,\%$ of the bottles hold at most $997$ ml.

Standardising reduces every normal probability to one table – that of $\Phi$.

Talk track: “$z$ = distance from the mean in standard deviations” – say it until it sticks. Work the milk example on the board: subtract $\mu$, divide by $\sigma$, look up $\Phi(1)$, use symmetry. Point ahead: the standardised mean (and later the test statistic) is exactly this move applied to $\bar X$, with the standard error instead of $\sigma$.

Exercise – normal distribution: fill volume

A bottling plant fills orange juice into 1-litre bottles. The fill volume $X$ (in ml) is normally distributed with $\mu=1000$ and $\sigma=10$, i.e. $X\sim N(1000,\,10^2)$. The legal minimum content is $985$ ml.

  1. What share of the bottles is underfilled, $P(X<985)$?
  2. With what probability does a bottle contain between $990$ and $1015$ ml?
  3. Which fill volume $x_{0.95}$ is exceeded by only $5\,\%$ of the bottles?
  4. The share of underfilled bottles is to be cut to at most $1\,\%$. To which setpoint $\mu$ must the plant be adjusted if $\sigma=10$ stays the same?

Hint: standardise, $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$, and use the $\Phi$ table.

Talk track: give 5–7 minutes. Insist on a sketch of the bell curve for every part – a) and b) are areas (forwards), c) and d) are quantiles (backwards). Part d) is the business punchline: the safety margin costs juice in every single bottle.

Solution (1/3) – fill volume: probabilities

Rules: $P(X\le x)=\Phi\bigl(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\bigr)$;  $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$;  $P(a\le X\le b)=\Phi(z_b)-\Phi(z_a)$.

\begin{align*} \text{a)}\quad P(X<985) &= \Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{985-1000}{10}\Bigr)=\Phi(-1.5)\\ &=1-\Phi(1.5)=1-0.9332=\mathbf{0.0668}\\[10pt] \text{b)}\quad P(990\le X\le 1015) &=\Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{1015-1000}{10}\Bigr)-\Phi\Bigl(\tfrac{990-1000}{10}\Bigr) =\Phi(1.5)-\Phi(-1)\\ &=0.9332-(1-0.8413)=0.9332-0.1587=\mathbf{0.7745} \end{align*}

Interpretation

About $6.7\,\%$ of the bottles are underfilled; about $77.5\,\%$ contain between $990$ and $1015$ ml.

Solution (2/3) – fill volume: quantile and setpoint

Rules: quantile $x_p=\mu+\sigma z_p$ (search $p$ inside the table);  $z_{1-p}=-z_p$.

\begin{align*} \text{c)}\quad &\Phi(z)=0.95\ \Rightarrow\ z_{0.95}=1.645 \ \Rightarrow\ x_{0.95}=1000+1.645\cdot10=\mathbf{1016.45\ ml}\\[4pt] \text{d)}\quad &P(X<985)=0.01\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac{985-\mu}{10}=z_{0.01}=-z_{0.99}\approx-2.33 \quad(\text{table: }\Phi(2.33)=0.9901)\\ &\Rightarrow\ \mu=985+2.33\cdot10=\mathbf{1008.3\ ml} \quad(\text{exact } z_{0.99}=2.3263:\ 1008.26\text{ ml}) \end{align*}

Estimators are random variables

The sample $X_1,\dots,X_n$ is random, so anything computed from it – $\bar X$, $s$, and later the test statistics – is itself a random variable.

  • Its distribution over all possible samples is the sampling distribution – so $\bar X$ and $s$ each have their own.

The standard error: how much $\bar X$ wobbles

Definition

The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of an estimator – the spread of $\bar X$ from sample to sample, not the spread of the raw data.

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\bar X)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\quad \Bigl(\text{or }\tfrac{s}{\sqrt n}\text{ when }\sigma\text{ is estimated}\Bigr). \]

  • It is the natural ruler for inference: we will measure how many standard errors $\bar x$ lies away from a reference value – this standardised quantity reappears as the test statistic in Section 3.
  • Larger $n$ $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE $\Rightarrow$ narrower CI and sharper test.

Standard deviation = spread of the data;    standard error = spread of the estimate.

The central limit theorem – intuition

Our inference rests on $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$. But real data – waiting times, incomes, processing times – are often skewed. Does the recipe still work?

  • Draw $n$ independent values from any distribution (skewed, flat, bimodal) with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.
  • Their mean $\bar X$ is approximately normal once $n$ is large enough – the shape of the original distribution washes out.
  • Why? In an average, unusually high and unusually low values partly cancel out; what remains is a symmetric bell around $\mu$.

Whatever the shape of the data: for large $n$ the sample mean is approximately normal, with centre $\mu$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$.

Talk track: ask first – “waiting times at a checkout are skewed; is the AVERAGE waiting time of 50 customers skewed too?” Let them guess, then show the simulation on the next slide. Keep the distinction sharp: the CLT is about averages (and sums), never about single observations.

The central limit theorem – formal statement

Central limit theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy)

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with $E(X_i)=\mu$ and $0<\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty$. Then

\[ Z_n=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ \xrightarrow{\;d\;}\ N(0,1) \quad (n\to\infty), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \bar X\ \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim}\ N\Bigl(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Bigr). \]

For large $n$ the standardised mean behaves like a standard normal variable – whatever the distribution of the single values.

  • $\xrightarrow{d}$ = convergence in distribution: the CDF of $Z_n$ approaches $\Phi$.
  • Rule of thumb: $n\gtrsim 30$ is usually enough; strongly skewed data need more.
  • For normal data, $\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$ holds exactly, for every $n$.
  • The CLT is about $\bar X$ – it does not make the single values normal.
Talk track: connect to what follows – every CI and test in this part standardises $\bar X$ like $Z_n$; the CLT is the licence to do so for non-normal data with $n\gtrsim30$ (assumptions checklist later). Typical exam trap: “by the CLT the data are normal” – no, only the mean is.

A single estimate is not enough

Intuition

From the sample we compute the mean $\bar x$. But it almost never hits the true value $\mu$ exactly – a second sample would give a different value.

  • Instead of a single point we report a range of values that are compatible with the data.
  • The more precise our estimate, the narrower this range.

A confidence interval reports the estimate together with its uncertainty – a range of plausible values for $\mu$. (It is not a probability statement about where $\mu$ lies – see the interpretation slides.)

How we read probability: the frequentist view

Fixed vs. random

  • Parameters $\mu,\sigma$ are fixed (unknown) constants – not random.
  • The data – and all we build from them ($\bar X$, $s$, $Z_n$, the CI bounds, later the test results) – are random.

What “probability” means here

The long-run relative frequency of an event over (hypothetically) very many repeated samples.

“$95\,\%$ confidence” and “$5\,\%$ significance” describe the procedure across repeated samples – not one particular interval and not one particular dataset.

How does the mean fluctuate? – formally

Sampling distribution of the sample mean

For $X_i \sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$ i.i.d., the sampling distribution of the random variable $\bar X$ is

\[ \bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\,\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z_n := \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1). \]

Both results are from the Foundations ($\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)= \sigma^2/n$, standardisation). New is the probability statement below – the building block of the confidence interval.

Two-sided probability statement

\[ P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \le Z_n \le z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha \]

With probability $1-\alpha$, $Z_n$ lies between the two quantiles $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}$.

Derivation (1/2): rearrange step by step

Start from the pivot $Z_n=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)$, which lies within $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}$ with probability $1-\alpha$:

\begin{align*} 1-\alpha &= P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\ \le\ \tfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ \le\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small starting point}\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ \bar X-\mu\ \le\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small multiply all 3 parts by }\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}>0\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(-\bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ -\mu\ \le\ -\bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small subtract }\bar X\text{ everywhere} \end{align*}

Multiplying by a positive number keeps the “$\le$” signs; we only used equivalence steps, so the probability stays exactly $1-\alpha$.

Talk track: the whole derivation is just “solve the inequality for $\mu$”. Announce that upfront so the algebra has a destination. Emphasise at the end of (2/2): the randomness sits in the BOUNDS (they contain $\bar X$), $\mu$ never moves – that is what later justifies the careful CI interpretation.

Derivation (2/2): from $-\mu$ to the interval

\begin{align*} 1-\alpha &= P\!\left(\bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \ge\ \mu\ \ge\ \bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small multiply by }(-1):\ \text{signs flip}\\[4pt] &= P\!\left(\bar X-z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \le\ \mu\ \le\ \bar X+z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right) && \rhd\ \text{\small just read it left-to-right} \end{align*}

Confidence interval with known variance:

\[ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[\bar{X}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Multiplying an inequality by $-1$ reverses “$\le$” into “$\ge$” – that is the only subtle step. What is random are the bounds (they contain $\bar X$); $\mu$ itself is a fixed unknown.

The formula in words

\[ \text{CI} = \big[\;\underbrace{\bar{X}}_{\text{estimate}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{z_{1-\alpha/2}}_{\substack{\text{critical}\\\text{value}}} \cdot \underbrace{\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}_{\text{standard error}}\;\big] \]

  • Centre $\bar X$: our best estimate of $\mu$.
  • Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: how strongly $\bar X$ fluctuates.
  • Critical value $z_{1-\alpha/2}$: how many standard errors we go to the left/right ($1.96$ at $95\,\%$).

Milk example: calculation

Given: $\bar{x}=1001$, $\sigma=3$, $n=9$, $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

\begin{align*} \text{margin of error } e &= z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = 1.96\cdot\frac{3}{\sqrt{9}} = 1.96\\[2pt] \text{lower bound} &= 1001 - 1.96 = 999.04\\ \text{upper bound} &= 1001 + 1.96 = 1002.96 \end{align*}

Result

\[ \text{CI}_{0.95} = \bigl[\,999.04\,;\,1002.96\,\bigr]\,\text{ml} \]

The hypothesised target value $\mu_0 = 1000$ lies inside the interval $\Rightarrow$ no evidence against a correctly working machine.

What does “95 % confidence” mean?

 

Of $20$ computed $95\,\%$ intervals, on average $\approx 19$ cover the true value $\mu$ (red line). The confidence level refers to the procedure across many samples.

Each row: one sample of $n=9$ bottles and its CI. Draw new samples or run 50 rounds – the long-run share settles near the confidence level.

A common misconception

Careful with the interpretation

“With $95\,\%$ probability $\mu$ lies in this particular interval” is, strictly speaking, wrong.

  • $\mu$ is a fixed (unknown) number – it either is or is not inside the particular interval.
  • What is random are the bounds of the interval, not $\mu$.
  • Correct: “The procedure yields, in $95\,\%$ of samples, an interval that covers $\mu$.”

Three correct ways to read a confidence interval

  1. Coverage (the procedure). The method produces intervals that contain the fixed true $\mu$ in a fraction $(1-\alpha)$ of all samples.
  2. Compatibility. The interval is the range of values for $\mu$ that are consistent with the observed data.
  3. Duality with the test. It is exactly the set of null values $\mu_0$ that a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test would not reject.

All three avoid the trap of putting a probability on this one interval: $\mu$ is fixed, the interval is what is random.

Planning the sample size

How many bottles must the dairy measure so that the $95\,\%$ CI is at most $\pm e$ wide – e.g. $\pm1$ ml?

Solve the margin of error for $n$

\[ e=z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sqrt n=\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{e} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\ \ge\ \Bigl(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{e}\Bigr)^{2} \quad\text{(round \emph{up})} \]

  • $n$ grows with the square of $z\sigma/e$: halving the margin of error needs four times as many observations.
  • Higher confidence (larger $z$) or a larger spread $\sigma$ also cost sample size.
  • $\sigma$ unknown? Plan with an estimate from a pilot study or from past data.

Decide on the precision you need before collecting data – the formula tells you what it costs.

More realistic: the variance is unknown

Problem

In practice $\sigma^{2}$ is almost never known. We must also estimate it from the sample.

Estimator of the variance

\[ s^{2} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^{2} \]

  • We replace $\sigma$ by $s$ in the test statistic.
  • This costs accuracy – and changes the distribution (see next slide).

Why the $t$-distribution? – intuitively and graphically

Standardised statistic (normal data)

\[ T_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \]

  • $s$ is itself a random variable.
  • $T_n$ is a ratio of two random quantities $\Rightarrow$ heavier tails.
  • For large $n$, $s$ barely varies, so $t_{n-1}\to N(0,1)$.

Unknown $\sigma$ $\Rightarrow$ use $t_{n-1}$: larger quantiles, so the CI is wider on average.

Milk example with unknown variance

Confidence interval

\[ \text{CI} = \left[\bar{X} - t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X} + t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right] \]

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $n=9$, $\alpha=0.05$, $s = 3.7749$, $t_{0.975;\,8} = 2.3060$.

\begin{align*} e &= 2.3060\cdot\frac{3.7749}{\sqrt{9}} = 2.9017\\ \text{CI}_{0.95} &= [\,1001\pm 2.9017\,] = [\,998.10\,;\,1003.90\,] \end{align*}

Summary and outlook

  • Confidence intervals quantify the uncertainty of a point estimate.
  • Known variance: quantiles of $N(0,1)$; unknown: of $t_{n-1}$.
  • The required precision fixes the sample size: $n\ge(z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/e)^2$.
  • Bridge to the test: if the CI contains the hypothesised value $\mu_0$, there is no evidence against $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ – otherwise there is.

What is a hypothesis test? – intuition

The basic idea, as in court

An assumption (the null hypothesis $H_0$) is held true until the data speak strongly enough against it.

  • $H_0$ = “innocent” (status quo).
  • We reject $H_0$ only on convincing evidence.
  • Little evidence $\Rightarrow$ acquittal for lack of evidence – not “proven innocent”.

Milk

$H_0:\mu = 1000$ – “the machine works correctly”. Only a clear deviation from $1000$ makes us doubt this.

The two hypotheses

Structure

\[ H_0:\mu = \mu_0 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1:\mu \neq \mu_0 \]

  • We always argue under “$H_0$ is true”.
  • Why unequal? $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ fixes a single value, so the test statistic has one definite distribution we can compute with; $H_1$ is a whole range, with no single distribution.
  • Two outcomes: keep $H_0$ or reject it.

What you want to establish statistically goes into $H_1$.

Which hypothesis goes where?

Rule of thumb

The statement you want to find evidence for – the “more important” claim – is formulated as $H_1$, because only the Type I error (wrongly rejecting $H_0$) can be controlled via $\alpha$.

You want evidence that …$H_0$$H_1$
the supplier's fill weight deviates from the spec$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$
the mean processing time changed after the update$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$
the mean daily revenue differs from the plan figure$\mu = \mu_0$$\mu \neq \mu_0$

Bridge: confidence interval and test are the same

Testing with the CI

If $\mu_0$ lies inside the confidence interval, $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ is not rejected – if it lies outside, it is.

Equivalence – same algebra, two readings

“$\mu_0$ inside the CI” means $|\bar x-\mu_0|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt n$, which rearranges to $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$ – exactly the non-rejection condition. So a test (level $\alpha$) and a CI (level $1-\alpha$) always agree.

The CI shows all $\mu_0$ that you would not reject.

The duality on the number line

 
 
 
 

$\mu_0$ inside the CI $\Leftrightarrow$ keep $H_0$;   outside $\Leftrightarrow$ reject. (Both at known $\sigma$, $\alpha=0.05$.)

Drag the red $\mu_0$ marks: the verdict and $|Z_n|$ change together.

Talk track: “the CI is the set of all null values that survive the test.” Walk both lines: milk – target inside, nothing to complain about; cream – target outside, the data are incompatible with 250 g. Point out the CI is centred at $\bar x$, never at $\mu_0$.

One picture, two readings

  • Distribution reading: the central blue region holds $95\,\%$ of the $Z_n$ distribution (in $95\,\%$ of samples $Z_n$ lands here).
  • Test reading: blue = acceptance region ($=$ non-rejection region), orange = rejection region ($\alpha$).
  • Test statistic in the orange tail $\Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$.

“Acceptance region” is conventional for the non-rejection region: landing there means we fail to reject $H_0$, not that we accept it as true.

Exercise – hypotheses and deciding with the CI

A bank plans its staff with a mean processing time of $\mu_0=30$ min per loan application. Longer times (bottlenecks) and shorter times (overcapacity) matter equally. From experience $\sigma=6$ min is known. A sample of $n=36$ applications gives $\bar x=32.8$ min;  $\alpha=0.05$.

  1. State $H_0$ and $H_1$.
  2. Compute the $95\,\%$ CI for $\mu$.
  3. Decide about $H_0$ with the CI.
  4. Which of the planning values $\mu_0=31$, $33$ and $35$ min would a two-sided test at level $5\,\%$ reject?

Hint: $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

Talk track: four minutes. Part a) practises “the deviation you want to detect goes into $H_1$”; part d) is the payoff of the duality – one interval answers the test for every $\mu_0$ at a glance, without a single new calculation.

Solution – hypotheses and deciding with the CI

Rule: $\mu_0$ inside the $(1-\alpha)$-CI $\Leftrightarrow$ $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ keep $H_0$.

  1. $H_0:\mu=30$  vs.  $H_1:\mu\neq30$ – both directions count; the deviation to be detected goes into $H_1$.
  2. $\mathrm{SE}=6/\sqrt{36}=6/6=1$,  $e=1.96\cdot1=1.96$,  $\text{CI}_{0.95}=[\,32.8\pm1.96\,]=\mathbf{[\,30.84\,;\,34.76\,]}$ min.
  3. $30\notin\text{CI}$ $\Rightarrow$ $H_0$ is rejected: the mean processing time deviates from the planning value. (Cross-check: $|Z_n|=|32.8-30|/1=2.8>1.96$.)
  4. One look at the CI suffices: $31$ and $33$ lie inside $[\,30.84\,;\,34.76\,]$ $\Rightarrow$ keep;  $35$ lies outside $\Rightarrow$ reject.

A CI answers the two-sided test for every hypothesised value $\mu_0$ at once.

The critical value – the bar you must clear

Plain idea

The critical value is a fixed threshold on the test statistic. It splits all possible outcomes into two zones:

Keep $H_0$

Statistic inside the threshold – the deviation is small enough to pass as ordinary chance.

Reject $H_0$

Statistic beyond the threshold – too extreme to be chance at level $\alpha$.

Talk track: “A critical value is just a line in the sand on the test statistic.” Use the high-jump image – $\alpha$ sets the bar height; smaller $\alpha$ = higher bar = harder to reject. Common student confusion: thinking a larger statistic is automatically “better” – it only matters relative to the bar. Anchor: the acceptance region is everything inside the bar(s).

The test recipe (known variance)

1. Hypotheses
$H_0:\mu=\mu_0$
$H_1:\mu\neq\mu_0$
→
2. Test statistic
$Z_n=\dfrac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$
→
3. Critical value
$z_{1-\alpha/2}$
→
$|Z_n|>z_{1-\alpha/2}$?
yes: reject $H_0$
no: keep $H_0$
  • Key move: plug the hypothesised $\mu_0$ in for the unknown $\mu$ (the same $Z_n$ as for the CI, only with $\mu_0$ instead of $\mu$) – if $H_0$ holds then $Z_n\sim N(0,1)$, so a $|Z_n|$ that would be very unusual for $N(0,1)$ is evidence against $H_0$.
  • $|Z_n|\le z_{1-\alpha/2}$: acceptance (non-rejection) region; beyond it: rejection region (area $\alpha$ under $H_0$). Fix $\alpha$ in advance.

Milk test with known variance

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $\sigma=3$, $n=9$, $\mu_0=1000$, $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.975}=1.96$.

\begin{align*} Z_n &= \frac{1001-1000}{3/\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{1} = 1.000\\[2pt] |Z_n| &= 1.000 \;<\; 1.96 = z_{0.975} \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\mu=1000$ is not rejected – no evidence against a correctly working machine.

Same result as the CI: $\mu_0=1000 \in [999.04\,;\,1002.96]$.

Test with unknown variance

Only two changes to the recipe

\[ T_n = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}\ \text{under } H_0, \qquad \text{rejection: } |T_n| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}. \]

  • $\sigma \to s$ (estimated) and $z \to t_{n-1}$.
  • Assumption: data (approximately) normally distributed or $n \ge 30$ (central limit theorem).

Milk test with unknown variance

Data: $\bar{x}=1001$, $s=3.7749$, $n=9$, $\mu_0=1000$, $\alpha=0.05$, $t_{0.975;\,8}=2.3060$.

\begin{align*} T_n &= \frac{1001-1000}{3.7749/\sqrt{9}} = \frac{1}{1.2583} = 0.7947\\[2pt] |T_n| &= 0.7947 \;<\; 2.3060 = t_{0.975;\,8} \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\mu=1000$ is not rejected. (More cautious $t$-quantile $2.31$ instead of $1.96$ – the result is nevertheless the same here.)

Before you test – the assumptions checklist

  1. Random sample, independent observations – the $X_i$ are i.i.d. draws from the population. (No clustering, no repeated measurements of the same unit.)
  2. Distribution: data approximately normal or $n\gtrsim 30$ – then the CLT (Foundations) makes $\bar X$ approximately normal.
  3. Spread: $\sigma$ known $\to$ $z$-test;  $\sigma$ estimated by $s$ $\to$ $t$-test with $\mathrm{df}=n-1$.
  4. Question fixed in advance: $\mu_0$, the direction (one-/two-sided) and $\alpha$ are chosen before seeing the results.

If 1.–2. fail, the stated $\alpha$ is not honest: the $5\,\%$ false-alarm guarantee only holds under the model assumptions.

Talk track: a test is a measurement instrument – the checklist is its calibration. Point 1 is the one most often violated in practice (convenience samples, duplicated units). For point 2, with $n=9$ as in the milk example we genuinely rely on approximate normality of fill volumes; mention a histogram or QQ-plot as the quick diagnostic. Point 4 anticipates the one-sided tests (appendix): direction-after-data doubles the true $\alpha$.

Hypothesis test with Python – ttest_1samp

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
milk = np.array([1005, 1001, 997, 1000, 1001, 998, 999, 999, 1009])
res = stats.ttest_1samp(milk, popmean=1000)    # two-sided by default
ci = res.confidence_interval(confidence_level=0.95)
print(f"t = {res.statistic:.4f}, df = {res.df}, p = {res.pvalue:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI: [{ci.low:.2f}, {ci.high:.2f}]")

95% CI: [998.10, 1003.90]

Exercise – delivery time (complete $t$-test by hand)

A logistics provider contractually guarantees an online shop a mean delivery time of $\mu_0=48$ hours. The shop's controller wants to check whether the actual mean delivery time deviates from this – both directions matter (too slow: customer complaints; too fast: the shop could book a cheaper, slower tariff). For $n=8$ randomly selected shipments she records the delivery time (in h):

\[ 51,\ 39,\ 49,\ 41,\ 47,\ 43,\ 45,\ 45 \]

Delivery times are approximately normal, $\sigma$ unknown, $\alpha=0.05$.

  1. Compute $\bar x$ and $s$.
  2. State $H_0$/$H_1$, compute $T_n$ and decide with the critical value.
  3. Compute the $95\,\%$ CI for $\mu$ and show that it agrees with the test decision.

Hint: $t_{0.975;\,7}=2.3646$.

Solution (1/4) – delivery time: key figures

Rule: $\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_i$,    $s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$.

$x_i$5139494147434545$\sum=360$
$x_i-\bar x$6$-6$4$-4$2$-2$00$\sum=0$
$(x_i-\bar x)^2$363616164400$\sum=112$
\begin{align*} \bar x &= \frac{360}{8}=\mathbf{45\ h}, \qquad s^2=\frac{112}{8-1}=\frac{112}{7}=16 \;\Longrightarrow\; s=\mathbf{4\ h}\\[2pt] \text{standard error:}\quad \frac{s}{\sqrt n}&=\frac{4}{\sqrt8} =\frac{4}{2.8284}=\mathbf{1.4142\ h} \end{align*}

The shipments took $45$ h on average; single values scatter with $4$ h, the sample mean only with $1.41$ h.

Solution (2/4) – delivery time: test with the critical value

Hypotheses (two-sided; the direction follows from the question, not from the data):

\[ H_0:\mu=48 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1:\mu\neq48 \]

Rule: $\sigma$ unknown $\Rightarrow$ $t$-test; reject if $|T_n|>t_{1-\alpha/2;\,n-1}=t_{0.975;\,7}=2.3646$.

\begin{align*} T_n &= \frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}=\frac{45-48}{1.4142} =\frac{-3}{1.4142}=\mathbf{-2.1213}\\[2pt] |T_n| &= 2.1213 \;<\; 2.3646 \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\mu=48$ is not rejected (narrowly). The observed deviation of $3$ h amounts to just over $2$ standard errors – with only $n=8$ shipments this is still compatible with chance; a breach of contract is not statistically established.

Solution (3/4) – delivery time: CI and duality

Rule: $\text{CI}_{0.95}=\bigl[\bar x\pm t_{0.975;\,7}\,s/\sqrt n\bigr]$.

\begin{align*} e &= 2.3646\cdot1.4142=3.344\\ \text{CI}_{0.95} &= [\,45\pm3.344\,]=\mathbf{[\,41.66\,;\,48.34\,]}\ \text{h} \end{align*}

Duality CI $\leftrightarrow$ test

$\mu_0=48\in[\,41.66\,;\,48.34\,]$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $|T_n|\le t_{0.975;\,7}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ keep $H_0$ – the same algebra, two readings. That $48$ lies just inside the upper bound mirrors the narrow $|T_n|=2.12$ vs. $2.36$.

Mean delivery times between $41.7$ h and $48.3$ h are compatible with the data – the contractual $48$ h are (just) among them.
