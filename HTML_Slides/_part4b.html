From describing the data to the regression model

Now: an idea of how the data come about

So far the OLS line describes the cloud of points. From now on we assume that the data are generated as

\[ y_i \;=\; \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i}_{\text{systematic part}} \;+\; \underbrace{u_i}_{\text{error term}}, \qquad i=1,\dots,n . \]

$\beta_0,\ \beta_1$
true, unknown coefficients; $\hat{\beta}_0$, $\hat{\beta}_1$ are their estimates from the data.
$u_i$
everything that $x$ does not explain (other influences, chance) – not observable.
$\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$
the residual: the estimate of $u_i$ computed from the data.

Requirement for the error term

The $u_i$ should contain no systematic information about $Y$ – everything explainable belongs in the linear part. We can check this only with the residuals: the residual analysis (Appendix A.3–A.5).

What do we assume about the error term?

Model $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i$: the standard assumptions concern the unobservable error term $u_i$.

AssumptionFormalIn words
Expected value zero$E(u_i) = 0$No systematic error: on average the observations lie on the true line – for every $x$.
Homoscedasticity$\operatorname{Var}(u_i) = \sigma^{2}$The spread around the line is the same everywhere (independent of $x_i$).
No autocorrelation$\operatorname{Cov}(u_i,u_j) = 0$ ($i \ne j$)The errors are pairwise uncorrelated (e.g. from month to month).
Normal distribution$u_i \sim N(0,\sigma^{2})$The errors scatter bell-shaped around zero – required for confidence intervals and tests (this section).

The problem

The assumptions are not self-evident and must be checked – with the residuals $\hat{u}_i$, our observable estimates of the unobservable $u_i$ (residual plot and QQ-plot: Appendix A.3–A.5).

Why regression coefficients carry uncertainty

Intuition (as in Part 3)

The nine stores are only a sample. Other stores – or the same stores in another week – would give slightly different sales, hence a slightly different line.

  • The true coefficients $\beta_0,\beta_1$ (population line) are fixed but unknown.
  • The estimates $\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1$ are computed from a random sample – they are random variables with a sampling distribution, exactly like $\bar X$ in Part 3.
  • Questions: How much does $\hat\beta_1$ wobble? Is the true slope possibly $0$? Which values of $\beta_1$ are compatible with the data?

The tools are the same as for the mean: standard error, $t$-test, confidence interval and $p$-value.

Population line vs. sample lines

Simulation with the lamp prices: (a) every sample gives its own OLS line around the fixed population line; (b) over $5000$ samples the slopes $\hat\beta_1$ are normally distributed around $\beta_1$ – their standard deviation is the standard error.

Sampling distribution of the slope

Result (under the four assumptions on $u_i$)

\[ \hat\beta_1 \sim N\!\left(\beta_1,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\right), \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}} =\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2}} . \]

  • $E(\hat\beta_1)=\beta_1$: the estimator is unbiased – on average it hits the true slope.
  • Less noise ($\sigma\downarrow$) $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE.
  • More spread in $x$ ($S_{xx}\uparrow$) $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE: a long “lever arm” fixes the slope precisely.
  • More observations increase $S_{xx}$ $\Rightarrow$ smaller SE.

Compare Part 3: $\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n$. Here the role of $n$ is taken by $S_{xx}$.

Estimating $\sigma$: the residual standard error

Problem

The error variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown – as $\sigma$ in Part 3. We estimate it from the residuals.

Residual standard error

\[ \hat\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\text{RSS}}{n-2}} =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat u_i^{\,2}}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_0)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}} . \]

  • Why $n-2$? Two parameters ($\beta_0,\beta_1$) were estimated – two degrees of freedom are used up (Part 1: one parameter $\bar x$ $\Rightarrow$ $n-1$).
  • $\hat\sigma$ is measured in the units of $y$: the typical size of a residual.

Standard errors – lamp example

Given: $n=9$, $\text{RSS}=16.733$, $S_{xx}=1500$, $\bar x=100$.

Calculation

\begin{align*} \hat\sigma &= \sqrt{\frac{16.733}{9-2}}=\sqrt{2.3905}=\mathbf{1.5461}\\[3pt] \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1) &= \frac{1.5461}{\sqrt{1500}}=\frac{1.5461}{38.730}=\mathbf{0.03992}\\[3pt] \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_0) &= 1.5461\sqrt{\frac19+\frac{100^2}{1500}} =1.5461\cdot 2.6034=\mathbf{4.0252} \end{align*}

A typical store deviates by about $1.5$ lamps from the line; the slope $-0.4467$ is uncertain by about $\pm 0.04$ (one standard error).

The first question: is there any relationship at all?

Hypotheses

\[ H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1: \beta_1 \ne 0 \]

  • $H_0$: the price has no (linear) influence on the sales quantity – the population line is flat.
  • $H_1$: the price has some influence.
  • As in Part 3: what we want to establish goes into $H_1$.

Rejecting $H_0:\beta_1=0$ is what licenses us to interpret the slope at all – a slope that could just as well be zero tells us nothing.

$t$-test – lamp example

Data: $\hat\beta_1=-0.4467$, $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.03992$, $n=9$, $\alpha=0.05$, $t_{0.975;\,7}=2.3646$.

\begin{align*} T &= \frac{-0.4467-0}{0.03992} = -11.19\\[2pt] |T| &= 11.19 \;>\; 2.3646 = t_{0.975;\,7} \end{align*}

Decision

$H_0:\beta_1=0$ is rejected. The price has a significant influence on the sales quantity. The $p$-value is $p=2\,F_{t,7}(-11.19)=0.00001$.

The estimated slope lies more than eleven standard errors away from zero – far too far to be a chance result.

One-sided test for the slope

Economic theory gives a direction

A price-sales function should fall. We want evidence for a negative slope:

\[ H_0:\beta_1=0 \quad\text{vs.}\quad H_1:\beta_1<0 \qquad(\text{left-sided}) \]

Lamp example

Reject if $T<-t_{1-\alpha,\,n-2}=-t_{0.95;\,7}=-1.8946$:

\[ T=-11.19<-1.8946\ \Rightarrow\ \text{reject }H_0, \qquad p=F_{t,7}(-11.19)=0.000005 . \]

As in Part 3 (appendix)

Fix the direction before looking at the data; the one-sided $p$ is half the two-sided one if $T$ points towards $H_1$. statsmodels reports the two-sided $p$-value (P>|t|, reg.pvalues).

Before you test – the assumptions checklist

  1. Linear model is correct – residual plot without systematic pattern (Appendix A.3).
  2. Independent observations – no autocorrelation (e.g. not the same store measured repeatedly).
  3. Constant variance – no funnel in the residual plot.
  4. Normally distributed errors – QQ-plot (Appendix A.5); for large $n$ the central limit theorem makes the $t$-test approximately valid anyway.

If the assumptions fail, the standard errors – and with them every $t$-test, confidence interval and $p$-value – are not reliable. The point estimates $\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1$ may still be fine.

Confidence interval for $\beta_1$

Construction – exactly as for $\mu$ in Part 3

\[ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\beta_1) =\Bigl[\hat\beta_1-t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1),\; \hat\beta_1+t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\Bigr] \]

\[ \text{CI} = \big[\;\underbrace{\hat\beta_1}_{\text{estimate}} \;\pm\; \underbrace{t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}}_{\substack{\text{critical}\\\text{value}}} \cdot \underbrace{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)}_{\text{standard error}}\;\big] \]

For large $n$ the critical value is close to $1.96\approx2$: $\hat\beta_1\pm2\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)$ is a quick $95\,\%$ interval.

Confidence interval – lamp example

Given: $\hat\beta_1=-0.4467$, $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.03992$, $t_{0.975;\,7}=2.3646$.

\begin{align*} \text{margin of error } e &= 2.3646\cdot 0.03992 = 0.0944\\ \text{CI}_{0.95}(\beta_1) &= [\,-0.4467\pm0.0944\,] = \mathbf{[-0.5411;\,-0.3523]} \end{align*}

Interpretation

The procedure covers the true slope in $95\,\%$ of all samples. Values of $\beta_1$ between $-0.54$ and $-0.35$ lamps per euro are compatible with the data.

Duality with the test

$0\notin\text{CI}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $H_0:\beta_1=0$ is rejected;    $-0.5\in\text{CI}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $H_0:\beta_1=-0.5$ is kept.

Confidence intervals – interpretation pitfalls

Careful with the interpretation

“With $95\,\%$ probability $\beta_1$ lies in this particular interval $[-0.5411;\,-0.3523]$” is, strictly speaking, wrong.

  • $\beta_1$ is a fixed (unknown) number; the bounds of the interval are random.
  • Correct: “The procedure yields, in $95\,\%$ of samples, an interval that covers $\beta_1$.”

Intercept

The CI for $\beta_0$ is $[45.15;\,64.18]$. It is computed in the same way – but $\beta_0$ (sales at price $0$) has no substantive meaning here.

Significant $\neq$ important

The $p$-value measures evidence, not size

With many observations even a tiny slope becomes highly significant. The size of the effect is read from $\hat\beta_1$ and its confidence interval.

Two questions about a new $x_0$

At a new price $x_0$ the prediction is $\hat y_0=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_0$. How precise is it? It depends on the question:

Confidence interval

for the mean sales $E(Y\mid x_0)$ of all stores with price $x_0$:

\[ \hat y_0\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma \sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}} \]

Prediction interval

for the sales $Y_0$ of one new store with price $x_0$:

\[ \hat y_0\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,\hat\sigma \sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}} \]

The “$1+$” adds the error $u_0$ of the single store: a prediction interval is always wider and never shrinks below $\pm t\,\hat\sigma$, however large $n$ becomes.

Prediction – lamp example

Given: $\hat\sigma=1.5461$, $n=9$, $\bar x=100$, $S_{xx}=1500$, $t_{0.975;\,7}=2.3646$, new price $x_0=110$: $\hat y_0=54.67-0.4467\cdot110=5.53$.

Calculation

\begin{align*} \text{CI: }& 5.53\pm2.3646\cdot1.5461\sqrt{\tfrac19+\tfrac{100}{1500}} =5.53\pm1.54=[3.99;\,7.07]\\[2pt] \text{PI: }& 5.53\pm2.3646\cdot1.5461\sqrt{1+\tfrac19+\tfrac{100}{1500}} =5.53\pm3.97=[1.57;\,9.50] \end{align*}

Reading

At $110$ euros, stores sell on average between $4$ and $7$ lamps ($95\,\%$ CI); a single new store will sell between about $2$ and $9$ lamps ($95\,\%$ PI).

Confidence band vs. prediction band

  • Both bands are narrowest at $\bar x=100$ and widen towards the edges.
  • The prediction band contains most of the points; the confidence band does not need to.

Extrapolation

At $x_0=125$ euros the line predicts $\hat y_0=-1.17$ lamps – impossible. Outside the data range the linear model breaks down.

\[ \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{1.0}{\sqrt{17.5}}=0.239,\qquad T=\frac{-2}{0.239}=-8.37,\qquad |T|>2.776\ \Rightarrow\ \text{reject }H_0 \]

\[ \text{CI}_{0.95}=-2\pm2.776\cdot0.239=[-2.66;\,-1.34] \]

The annual loss of value lies between $1340$ and $2660$ euros; the interval excludes $0$, in line with the $t$-test.

Solution (3/3) – used cars from A to Z

(4) Prediction at $x_0=7$

$\hat y_0=22-2\cdot7=8$ and $(x_0-\bar x)^2=12.25$:

Common mistake

Dropping the “$1+$” under the root: $8\pm2.58$ is the confidence interval for the average $7$-year-old car – far too narrow for a single car.

Why one explanatory variable is rarely enough

Intuition

Sales do not depend on the price alone – advertising, season, location and competitors matter as well. Real questions involve several explanatory variables at once.

  • Multiple regression studies the effect of one variable while holding the others fixed (ceteris paribus).
  • Separate simple regressions cannot do this: if the explanatory variables move together, each simple slope quietly absorbs the effects of the others.

One joint model disentangles the effects – this is what makes multiple regression the workhorse of quantitative research.

The multiple linear regression model

Model with $p$ explanatory variables

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip} + u_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n, \]

with the same four assumptions on the error terms $u_i$ as before.

Interpretation of a coefficient

$\beta_j$ is the expected change in $Y$ when $X_j$ increases by one unit and all other explanatory variables are held fixed.

Advertising

\[ \texttt{sales}_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\texttt{TV}_i+\beta_2\,\texttt{radio}_i +\beta_3\,\texttt{newspaper}_i+u_i \]

Geometry: from a line to a plane

  • With two explanatory variables the fitted model $\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x_1+\hat\beta_2x_2$ is a plane.
  • The residuals are again the vertical distances of the points from the plane.
  • With $p$ variables: a “hyperplane” – no longer drawable, but the same idea.

Least squares with several variables

Same principle

Choose $\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1,\dots,\hat\beta_p$ such that the sum of squared residuals is minimal:

\[ \min_{\hat\beta_0,\dots,\hat\beta_p}\ \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1x_{i1}-\cdots-\hat\beta_px_{ip}\bigr)^2 . \]

  • The solution is unique as long as no variable is an exact linear combination of the others.
  • There is no simple “covariance over variance” formula any more – in matrix notation $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf y$, which software computes for us.
  • Residuals, RSS, TSS and $R^2=1-\text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$ are defined exactly as before.

$t$-test for a single coefficient

Same recipe – new degrees of freedom

\[ H_0:\beta_j=0 \quad\text{vs.}\quad H_1:\beta_j\neq0, \qquad T=\frac{\hat\beta_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)}\;\sim\;t_{n-p-1}\ \text{under }H_0 \]

  • $n-p-1$ degrees of freedom: $p+1$ coefficients were estimated (simple regression: $p=1$ $\Rightarrow$ $n-2$).
  • $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{RSS}/(n-p-1)}$; the standard errors are computed by software.
  • Reading of $H_0$: “$X_j$ adds nothing given the other variables in the model”.

Is any explanatory variable useful? – the $F$-test

Hypotheses

\[ H_0:\beta_1=\beta_2=\cdots=\beta_p=0 \quad\text{vs.}\quad H_1:\text{at least one }\beta_j\neq0 \]

$F$-statistic

\[ F=\frac{(\text{TSS}-\text{RSS})/p}{\text{RSS}/(n-p-1)} \;\sim\;F_{p,\,n-p-1}\ \text{under }H_0 \]

  • Numerator: explained variation per explanatory variable.
  • Denominator: unexplained variation per residual degree of freedom ($=\hat\sigma^2$).
  • Large $F$ $\Rightarrow$ the variables explain more than chance would $\Rightarrow$ reject $H_0$ (right-sided test).

$F$-test – advertising example

Given: $\text{TSS}=5417.15$, $\text{RSS}=556.83$, $n=200$, $p=3$.

\[ F=\frac{(5417.15-556.83)/3}{556.83/196}=\frac{1620.11}{2.8410}=\mathbf{570.3} \;\gg\;2.65=F_{0.95;\,3,\,196} \]

$H_0$ is rejected: at least one advertising medium is related to sales (Prob (F-statistic) $=1.58\cdot10^{-96}$, practically $0$).

Why not just several $t$-tests?

Multiple testing

With $p$ explanatory variables and $\alpha=0.05$ per test, even if no variable matters we expect about $0.05\cdot p$ “significant” $t$-tests by chance alone – with $p=20$ that is one false alarm per regression.

The $F$-test

One single test for all slopes together – its Type I error stays at $\alpha$, however many variables are in the model. Consult it first, then look at the individual $t$-tests.

Special case

In simple regression ($p=1$) the $F$-test and the two-sided $t$-test are identical: $F=T^2$ (lamp: $125.2=(-11.19)^2$).

The $F$-test with Python

Exercise – the $F$-test

A multiple regression with $p=3$ explanatory variables is fitted on $n=100$ observations, giving $\text{TSS}=1000$ and $\text{RSS}=100$.

  1. Compute $R^2$ and the $F$-statistic.
  2. Compare with $F_{0.95;\,3,\,96}=2.70$. What do you conclude?

Solution – the $F$-test

(1) $R^2$ and $F$

\[ R^2=1-\frac{100}{1000}=0.9,\qquad F=\frac{(1000-100)/3}{100/(100-3-1)}=\frac{300}{1.0417}=\mathbf{288} \]

(2) $288\gg2.70$ $\Rightarrow$ reject $H_0:\beta_1=\beta_2=\beta_3=0$: at least one variable is related to $Y$.

(3) Each $t$-test rejects a true $H_0$ with probability $0.05$, so about $100\cdot0.05=5$ variables would look significant purely by chance – which is why the $F$-test is consulted first.

Common mistake

Reading a significant $F$ as “all three variables matter”. It only says that at least one does; the $t$-tests show which.

$R^2$ and adjusted $R^2$

$R^2$ never decreases

Adding any variable – even pure noise – can only lower RSS, so $R^2$ never decreases. Comparing models by $R^2$ always favours the larger model.

Adjusted $R^2$

\[ \bar R^2=1-\frac{\text{RSS}/(n-p-1)}{\text{TSS}/(n-1)} =1-(1-R^2)\,\frac{n-1}{n-p-1} \]

Penalises each additional variable: $\bar R^2$ only rises if the new variable reduces RSS by more than chance would.

The residual standard error $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\text{RSS}/(n-p-1)}$ is penalised in the same way.

$K$ categories: use $K-1$ dummies

Rule

A variable with $K$ categories is coded with $K-1$ dummies; the remaining category is the baseline represented by the intercept.

Why not $K$ dummies? (dummy variable trap)

The $K$ dummies always sum to $1$ – exactly the column of the intercept. One variable would be an exact linear combination of the others, and least squares has no unique solution.

Credit data: region $\in\{$East, South, West$\}$, baseline East

\[ \widehat{\texttt{Balance}}=531.00-12.50\,D_{\text{South}}-18.69\,D_{\text{West}} \]

Both dummy coefficients are differences to East ($p=0.83$ and $p=0.77$): no evidence for regional differences.

“The slope is not significant, so $X$ has no effect.”No evidence found – the CI shows which effects are still compatible.
“In the multiple regression, $\hat\beta_j$ is the total effect of $X_j$.”It is the effect holding the other variables fixed.

Appendix: derivation (2/7) – derivative with respect to $\beta_0$

A single term: chain rule

\begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_0}\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)^{2} &= \underbrace{2\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)}_{\text{outer}} \cdot \underbrace{(-1)}_{\text{inner}} \;=\; -2\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr) \end{align*}

Inner function $y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i$: differentiated with respect to $\beta_0$ it leaves $-1$, because $y_i$ and $\beta_1 x_i$ do not depend on $\beta_0$.

All terms: sum rule

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_0} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} -2\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr) \;=\; -2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr) \]

The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives; the constant factor $-2$ moves in front of the sum.

Appendix: derivation (3/7) – derivative with respect to $\beta_1$

A single term: chain rule

\begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_1}\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)^{2} &= \underbrace{2\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)}_{\text{outer}} \cdot \underbrace{(-x_i)}_{\text{inner}} \;=\; -2\,x_i\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr) \end{align*}

Differentiated with respect to $\beta_1$: $\partial(-\beta_1 x_i)/\partial \beta_1 = -x_i$; $y_i$ and $\beta_0$ do not depend on $\beta_1$.

All terms: sum rule

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_1} \;=\; -2\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr) \]

Interim result

Both derivatives contain the residuals $y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i$ of the respective line.

Appendix: derivation (4/7) – first condition: the intercept

$\partial S / \partial \beta_0 = 0$  step by step

\begin{align*} -2\tsum (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) &= 0 && \mid\ \text{divide by } (-2) \\ \tsum (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) &= 0 && \mid\ \text{split the sum} \\ \tsum y_i - \tsum \beta_0 - \tsum \beta_1 x_i &= 0 && \mid\ \tsum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_0 = n\beta_0, \ \ \tsum \beta_1 x_i = \beta_1 \tsum x_i \\ \tsum y_i - n\beta_0 - \beta_1 \tsum x_i &= 0 && \mid\ + n\beta_0 \\ n\beta_0 &= \tsum y_i - \beta_1 \tsum x_i && \mid\ \text{divide by } n \\ \hat{\beta}_0 &= \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\,\bar{x} && \mid\ \text{as } \tfrac{1}{n}\tsum y_i = \bar{y}, \ \tfrac{1}{n}\tsum x_i = \bar{x} \end{align*}

Line 4 rearranged is the first normal equation $\sum y_i = n\beta_0 + \beta_1 \sum x_i$. Line 2 with the OLS values: $\sum \hat{u}_i = 0$ – the residuals of the OLS line sum to zero.

Appendix: derivation (5/7) – second condition: the slope

$\partial S / \partial \beta_1 = 0$  and insert the intercept

Numerator from (5/7): multiply out the cross-products

Second partial derivatives: differentiate (2) and (3) once more

\begin{align*} \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_0^{2}} &= -2\tsum(-1) = 2n & \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_1^{2}} &= -2\tsum x_i(-x_i) = 2\tsum x_i^{2} & \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_0\,\partial \beta_1} &= -2\tsum (-x_i) = 2\tsum x_i \end{align*}

Minimum in two variables: both conditions hold

\begin{align*} &\text{(1)}\quad \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_0^{2}} = 2n > 0 \\ &\text{(2)}\quad \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_0^{2}} \cdot \frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_1^{2}} - \Bigl(\frac{\partial^{2} S}{\partial \beta_0\,\partial \beta_1}\Bigr)^{2} = 2n \cdot 2\tsum x_i^{2} - \bigl(2\tsum x_i\bigr)^{2} \\ &\phantom{\text{(2)}\quad} = 4\bigl(n\tsum x_i^{2} - n^{2}\bar{x}^{2}\bigr) = 4n\bigl(\tsum x_i^{2} - n\bar{x}^{2}\bigr) = 4n\,S_{xx} > 0 && \mid\ \tsum x_i = n\bar{x} \end{align*}

Layout

The points $(\hat{y}_i,\,\hat{u}_i)$, $i = 1,\ldots,n$, are plotted.

Why $\hat{y}_i$ and not $x_i$?

$\hat{y}_i$ is just a linear transformation of $x_i$: the pattern stays the same (mirrored if $\hat{\beta}_1 < 0$). With $\hat{y}_i$ the plot also works with several explanatory variables.

What always holds

The OLS residuals add up to zero (first-order condition): $\sum \hat{u}_i = 0$. So the points always lie around the zero line as a whole – only their structure is informative.

Ideal: a structureless cloud of points in a band of constant width around the zero line. Then the line has captured everything systematic.

Outliers and leverage values

Squaring makes large deviations count heavily – single unusual points can therefore have a strong influence on the line. Two kinds of unusual points:

Outlier

A point with an unusually large residual $|\hat u_i|$ – unusual in $y$ (lamp: the store at $95$ euros).

High-leverage point

A point with an unusual $x$-value, far from $\bar x$ – it can pull the line towards itself.

Leverage value $h_i$

The leverage value measures how unusual the $x$-value of observation $i$ is – and thus how strongly this point can pull the line towards itself:

\[ h_i \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \;+\; \frac{(x_i - \bar{x})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_j - \bar{x})^{2}}, \qquad \frac1n\le h_i\le 1 . \]

The further $x_i$ lies from the mean $\bar{x}$, the larger $h_i$ becomes (a long “lever arm”).

Leverage values – rule of thumb

Rule of thumb for points with high leverage

Simple regression
Values with $h_i > \dfrac{4}{n}$ are flagged as high-leverage points.
Small samples
Higher threshold $h_i > \dfrac{6}{n}$.

(In multiple regression with $p$ explanatory variables: $h_i>2(p+1)/n$ – see A.7.)

Caution

A flagged point is a reason to inspect it (measurement error? special case?) and to compare the fit with and without it – not to delete it automatically. Every removal changes the estimate and must be justified substantively.

Is the error term normally distributed? The QQ-plot

Starting point

The fourth standard assumption is $u_i \sim N(0,\sigma^{2})$. It is needed for confidence intervals and tests (Section “Inference”) – exactly as in Part 3 for the mean. For computing and interpreting the OLS line it plays no role.

How can we check whether data – here the residuals $\hat{u}_i$ – could come from a normal distribution?

A histogram?

With $n = 9$ or $n = 15$ values the picture depends heavily on the classes: hardly any verdict is possible.

Better: compare quantiles

The QQ-plot (quantile-quantile plot) compares the sorted values with those expected under a normal distribution.

The idea: plot quantiles against each other

Construction

  1. Determine the same number of quantiles of both distributions (for samples: the sorted values).
  2. Plot the $i$-th quantile of one distribution against the $i$-th quantile of the other as a point.

Reading

Points on a straight line: both distributions have the same shape. Different location and spread only shift and tilt the line.

Reference line

The line through the pair of $25\,\%$ quantiles and the pair of $75\,\%$ quantiles: robust – outliers hardly move it.

The further the points deviate systematically from the line, the more dissimilar the shapes of the two distributions.

Residuals against the normal distribution: theoretical quantiles

$n = 5$: five strips with probability $\frac{1}{5}$ each; red: $z_i$ in the middle of each strip

Procedure

  1. Sort the residuals: $\hat{u}_{(1)} \le \ldots \le \hat{u}_{(n)}$.
  2. Assign each rank $i$ the plotting position $p_i = \frac{i - 0.5}{n}$.
  3. Theoretical quantile of the standard normal distribution: $z_i = \Phi^{-1}(p_i)$, i.e. $P(Z \le z_i) = p_i$.
  4. Plot the points $\bigl(z_i,\,\hat{u}_{(i)}\bigr)$.

$z_i$ is where the $i$-th smallest of $n$ values “should” lie under a normal distribution. Example $n = 5$: $z_i = -1.28,\ -0.52,\ 0,\ 0.52,\ 1.28$.

Typical deviations in the QQ-plot

Points close to the line, small random deviations
S-shape: left end below, right end above the line – more extreme values (outliers)
Arc: both ends above the line – long upper tail

Small deviations, especially at the ends, are normal for small $n$. Take systematic shapes such as an S or an arc seriously.

Residual analysis at a glance

Residuals $\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$   (always $\sum\hat u_i=0$)

  • Residual plot ($\hat y_i$ vs. $\hat u_i$): structureless band $\to$ line adequate; arc $\to$ wrong functional form; funnel $\to$ heteroscedasticity; waves (time order) $\to$ autocorrelation.
  • QQ-plot ($z_i=\Phi^{-1}\bigl(\frac{i-0.5}{n}\bigr)$ vs. $\hat u_{(i)}$): points on the line $\to$ normality plausible; S-shape $\to$ heavy tails; arc $\to$ skewness.

Influential points

Outlier: large $|\hat u_i|$. Leverage $h_i>4/n$ (simple regression): unusual $x$. Inspect, compare the fit with and without the point – do not delete it blindly.

When the effect of one variable depends on another

Interaction model

\[ y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\beta_2x_{i2}+\beta_3\,x_{i1}x_{i2}+u_i \]

The effect of $x_1$ is now $\beta_1+\beta_3x_2$ – it depends on $x_2$.

Advertising: sales ~ TV * radio

\[ \widehat{\texttt{sales}}=6.750+0.0191\,\texttt{TV}+0.0289\,\texttt{radio} +0.00109\,\texttt{TV}\cdot\texttt{radio} \]

Interaction: $T=20.7$, $p<0.0001$; $R^2$ rises from $0.897$ to $0.968$.

Hierarchy principle

If $x_1x_2$ is in the model, keep $x_1$ and $x_2$ as well – even if their own $t$-tests are not significant.

Interaction, drawn: synergy between TV and radio

  • Effect of $1000\,\$$ more TV budget: $0.0191+0.00109\cdot\texttt{radio}$, i.e. $0.030$ at radio $=10$, but $0.063$ at radio $=40$.
  • Parallel lines $=$ no interaction; lines that fan out $=$ synergy.

Exercise – interactions and the hierarchy principle

The interaction model for the advertising data gives

\[ \widehat{\texttt{sales}}=6.750+0.0191\,\texttt{TV}+0.0289\,\texttt{radio} +0.00109\,\texttt{TV}\cdot\texttt{radio}. \]

  1. Interpret the interaction coefficient $0.00109$ in words.
  2. Compute the effect of $1000\,\$$ more TV budget when the radio budget is $0$ and when it is $50$ (in $1000\,\$$).
  3. Suppose the TV main effect were not significant, but the interaction were. Should TV be dropped from the model?

Solution – interactions and the hierarchy principle

(1) The effect of TV on sales is not constant: it grows with the radio budget (and vice versa). Each additional unit of radio budget raises the TV slope by $0.00109$ – a synergy between the media.

(2) Effect of TV: $\;0.0191+0.00109\cdot\texttt{radio}$

\[ \texttt{radio}=0:\ 0.0191,\qquad \texttt{radio}=50:\ 0.0191+0.00109\cdot50=0.0736 \]

With a radio budget of $50$, TV is almost four times as effective.

(3) No – by the hierarchy principle, the main effects stay in the model whenever their interaction does.

Common mistake

Quoting $0.0191$ as “the” TV effect. With an interaction, it is only the TV effect at $\texttt{radio}=0$.

Outliers and leverage in multiple regression

Recall A.4: an outlier is unusual in $y$ (large residual $|\hat u_i|$), a high-leverage point is unusual in $x$.

What is new with several explanatory variables

“Unusual in $x$” now means an unusual combination of the explanatory variables (e.g. a young customer with a very high credit limit). Such a point can be invisible in the plots of $y$ against one variable at a time – the leverage $h_i$ detects it anyway.

Rule of thumb for the leverage

The leverages $h_i$ average $(p+1)/n$; flag points with $h_i>2(p+1)/n$  (simple regression: $4/n$).

Worst case

A point that is both unusual in $x$ and badly fitted in $y$ can dominate the fit – inspect it, compare the fit with and without it.

Collinearity

Definition

Two or more explanatory variables are (almost) linearly dependent – they carry nearly the same information.

When two variables move in lockstep (right), the data cannot separate their individual effects – holding one fixed while the other changes almost never happens.

Adding Rating (correlation $0.997$ with Limit) multiplies the standard error of Limit by about $12.5$ – the clearly relevant variable suddenly looks “insignificant”.

Collinearity does not spoil the predictions of the model; it spoils the attribution of the effect to individual variables.

Variance inflation factor (VIF)

Definition

\[ \operatorname{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}, \]

where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ of a regression of $x_j$ on all other explanatory variables.

  • $\operatorname{VIF}_j=1$: no collinearity; $\operatorname{VIF}_j>5$ (some authors: $10$): problematic.
  • $\sqrt{\operatorname{VIF}_j}$ is the factor by which collinearity inflates $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$.
  • Remedies: drop one of the redundant variables or combine them into one index.

Credit data (Age, Limit, Rating)

$\operatorname{VIF}_{\text{Age}}=1.01$,   $\operatorname{VIF}_{\text{Limit}}=160.6$,   $\operatorname{VIF}_{\text{Rating}}=160.7$   ($\sqrt{160.6}=12.7$ – close to the observed SE ratio $0.06296/0.00503=12.5$).

Exercise – a diagnostics case study

House prices are regressed on size, bedrooms, age and income of the area ($n=400$, $p=4$). The diagnostics show:

  • Residual plot: a clear U-shaped curve, and the spread of the residuals widens from left to right.
  • Leverage: one point has $h_i=0.30$.
  • VIF: size $8.9$, bedrooms $7.5$, age $1.3$, income $9.2$.

Name every problem and suggest a remedy for each.

Solution – a diagnostics case study

  1. U-shape – non-linearity: the linear model misses curvature. Remedy: add e.g. $\texttt{size}^2$ or transform a variable ($\log\texttt{size}$).
  2. Widening spread – heteroscedasticity: standard errors, $t$- and $F$-tests are unreliable. Remedy: transform the response, e.g. $\log(\texttt{price})$.
  3. Leverage: threshold $2(p+1)/n=10/400=0.025$; $h_i=0.30$ is twelve times as large. Remedy: inspect the point, compare the fit with and without it.
  4. Collinearity: VIFs of $7.5$–$9.2$ for size, bedrooms and income. Remedy: drop or combine redundant variables (e.g. keep size, drop bedrooms) and recompute the VIFs.

Common mistake

Deleting flagged points automatically. Diagnostics call for inspection and sensitivity checks – not for blind removal.

Appendix summary

  • Derivation: chain rule, normal equations, $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$ and $\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$; a minimum, as $4n\,S_{xx}>0$.
  • Least squares: any other line has $S=\text{RSS}+n(a+b\bar x)^2+b^2S_{xx}$ – the OLS line is the unique minimum.
  • Interactions $x_1x_2$: the effect of $x_1$ is $\beta_1+\beta_3x_2$; keep the main effects (hierarchy principle).
  • Leverage in multiple regression: flag $h_i>2(p+1)/n$ – inspect, do not delete blindly.
  • Collinearity: $\operatorname{VIF}_j=1/(1-R_j^2)>5$ inflates $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ by $\sqrt{\operatorname{VIF}_j}$; predictions stay fine, the attribution to single variables does not.
